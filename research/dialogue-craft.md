# Dialogue craft

The current Chapter 1 draft has zero lines of dialogue — every character is
described from outside rather than heard. This is a starting toolkit for
writing naturalistic, character-differentiated dialogue from Chapter 2
onward (the first chapter that requires it, per `manuscript/outline.md`).

## Core principles

**1. Dialogue is action, not information delivery.** Each line should be
something a character does to get what they want in the scene (reassure,
deflect, provoke, extract information), not a vehicle for exposition. If a
line's only job is to tell the reader a fact, look for a way to make a
character need to say it, resist saying it, or say it wrong.

**2. People rarely say exactly what they mean.** Subtext — what's actually
going on beneath the literal words — is where dialogue gets its charge.
Sarah and Michael's first meeting (Chapter 2) is a natural place for this:
neither has reason to be fully open, so the scene should be built on both
characters talking around what they actually want to know/say.

**3. Interruption, silence, and non-answers are dialogue too.** A character
who changes the subject, answers a question with a question, or trails off
is often more characterizing than a direct answer. This is especially
useful for Michael (guarded) and Emma (controlled) per
`voice-differentiation.md`.

**4. Cut the small talk that isn't doing double duty.** Real conversation
has filler; written dialogue should only keep filler that's also
characterizing or tension-building (e.g., a stalling tactic reads
differently than genuine chit-chat).

## Making six characters sound different in dialogue

Apply the same levers as narration (see `voice-differentiation.md`) but to
speech specifically:

- **Sentence length/completeness:** Jack and Emma likely speak in more
  complete, controlled sentences; Liam more fragmented and interruptive;
  Olivia terse and efficient (she doesn't have time for a long
  conversation); Michael sparing, sometimes answering with fewer words than
  the question warrants.
- **What they avoid saying.** A character's dialogue is defined as much by
  the questions they dodge as the ones they answer — useful for Emma's
  secret and Michael's past specifically, since both are hiding something
  the plot isn't ready to reveal yet.
- **Distinct verbal habits**, used sparingly: one recurring phrase, a
  tendency to answer questions literally vs. evasively, a habit of trailing
  off vs. one of finishing every thought precisely. Avoid over-using any
  single tic — it should read as a subtle pattern, not a catchphrase.
- **Avoid "on-the-nose" dialogue** where a character states their own
  emotional state aloud ("I feel so confined by my family") — this is the
  spoken-word version of the manuscript's existing tell-don't-show problem
  and should be avoided even more strictly in dialogue than in narration,
  since real people rarely narrate their own feelings accurately.

## Mechanics

- **Minimize dialogue tags.** "Said" is close to invisible and is usually
  enough; avoid adverb-heavy tags ("she said anxiously") — if the anxiety
  isn't legible from the line and the action around it, add a small
  physical beat instead of an adverb.
- **Use action beats instead of tags** to both attribute a line and keep
  the scene physically grounded: a small gesture, a pause, a glance away,
  placed before or after the line rather than a "said" tag every time.
- **Break up long speeches.** Real dialogue is rarely a monologue; give the
  other character room to react, interrupt, or redirect even in a scene
  that's mostly one character explaining something.
- **Match punctuation to voice.** Dashes for a character who gets cut off
  or interrupts herself; ellipses (used sparingly) for trailing off;
  short, period-heavy sentences for someone terse.

## First application: Chapter 2 (Sarah meets Michael)

This is the manuscript's first dialogue scene. Concretely: give both
characters something they want out of the exchange beyond politeness (Sarah
might be sizing him up as a subject to paint or as a threat to her routine;
Michael might be testing whether this town is safe to stay in), and let
neither fully get it, so the conversation has friction rather than reading
as a pleasant first-meeting montage.
