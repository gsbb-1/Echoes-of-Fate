# Show, don't tell

Practical techniques for dramatizing inner conflict instead of stating it,
aimed at the flat summary-narration in `manuscript/chapters/chapter-01.md`.

## Why the current draft "tells"

Every paragraph in Chapter 1 follows the same pattern: name the character,
give one physical action, then state the theme directly in an abstract
sentence — "she felt confined by the expectations of her family," "he kept
[his past] hidden behind a stoic facade," "a secret that threatened to
unravel the carefully constructed facade." These sentences are conclusions a
reader should be reaching on their own, not information handed to them. They
also happen nowhere — there's no scene, no stakes in the moment, so the
narrator has nothing to dramatize and falls back on summary.

## Techniques to use instead

**1. Externalize the feeling as a physical/behavioral symptom.**
Don't name the emotion; describe what the body or hands are doing that the
character herself might not notice. Tension shows up as a held breath, a
repeated small action, an unfinished sentence — not as the label "anxious."

**2. Let action contradict or complicate stated intention.**
A character who claims (even just to herself) that everything is fine but
is scrubbing the same spot on the counter for the third time reveals more
than a sentence declaring she's stressed. The gap between what's said/done
and what's felt is where "showing" lives.

**3. Use concrete, specific objects instead of abstractions.**
"Confined by family expectations" is abstract. A specific unopened letter
from a parent, a half-packed bag pushed under the bed, a scholarship
acceptance she never mailed — these are ownable, visual, and let the reader
infer the constraint themselves.

**4. Give the character a want and an obstacle *in the scene*, not in
narration.** Something has to be at stake in the two minutes we're watching
her, even if it's small (she's trying to finish a painting before a buyer
arrives and her hand won't stop shaking).

**5. Filter description through the character's specific attention, not a
neutral camera.** What a character notices, and in what order, characterizes
her — see `voice-differentiation.md`.

**6. Use dialogue and subtext rather than narrated backstory.** A single
exchange can carry more characterizing weight than a paragraph of exposition
— see `dialogue-craft.md`.

## Before / after (from the current Chapter 1 draft)

**Before:**
> Sarah was a talented artist, but she felt confined by the expectations of
> her family and the memories of a past she longed to escape.

This states the theme twice ("confined," "longed to escape") without a
single concrete detail attached to either.

**After (rewrite):**
> The canvas needed maybe twenty more minutes. Sarah didn't have twenty
> minutes — her mother's voicemail was still sitting unplayed on her phone,
> three days old now, and she knew without listening that it would end the
> way they all did: *just come home for the gallery opening, Sarah, it
> would mean so much to your father.* She set the brush down, picked the
> phone up, put it back down again without opening anything, and picked the
> brush back up.

The rewrite never says "confined" or "expectations." It shows an unopened
voicemail (concrete, ownable), a repeated small physical action (the
phone/brush hesitation), and lets the reader infer the family pressure and
Sarah's avoidance of it. It also gives the paragraph a live present-tense
tension (will she call back?) instead of only summarizing settled facts.

## Applying this across the manuscript

When revising each character's introduction, look for any sentence of the
shape "[Character] felt/was [abstract trait]" and ask: what visible, scene-
level action or object could make a reader arrive at that same conclusion
without being told?
