# Voice differentiation across six POV characters

Practical levers for making Sarah's chapters read differently from Jack's,
using the profiles in `characters/`. The goal isn't six wildly different
narrators (this is still close-third, not first-person pastiche) — it's six
consistently distinguishable filters on the same underlying prose style.

## The four levers

For each character, decide (and write down, ideally in `characters/*.md`)
a short answer to each:

1. **Sentence rhythm.** Short and clipped vs. long and winding. This alone
   does a lot of work even before word choice differs.
2. **Diction / vocabulary register.** Formal vs. colloquial; trade
   vocabulary vs. plain speech; whether the narration ever uses slang or
   profanity in free indirect style.
3. **What they notice first, and in what order**, when entering a space.
   This is the single most effective lever for close-third — the same room
   described through two characters should produce two different first
   details.
4. **Metaphor source.** The images a character's mind reaches for under
   stress should come from their own life/trade, not from a generic
   narrator's toolkit.

## Suggested voice notes per character

- **Sarah (artist):** notices color, light, composition, and texture before
  anything else — a room registers to her in visual terms ("the light coming
  in wrong," "his jacket the color of wet slate") before it registers as
  information. Sentences can run longer when she's absorbed in work, then
  fracture into fragments when interrupted or anxious. Metaphors come from
  paint, canvas, negative space.
- **Michael (guarded newcomer):** short, withheld sentences; narration stays
  close to observable fact and avoids interiority the character himself is
  avoiding — he notices exits, hands, and tells (small physical cues on
  other people) before faces. Free indirect style should often *not* name
  what he's feeling, mirroring his own avoidance — let action carry it (ties
  directly to `show-dont-tell.md`).
  Sentences resist self-reflection: he deflects onto observation of others
  rather than his own state.
- **Emma (businesswoman with a secret):** precise, controlled diction —
  complete sentences, minimal fragments, because control is her whole
  strategy. Notices status markers: how people are dressed, who's late, who
  holds eye contact. Under stress, the control cracks in small, specific
  ways (a recount of exact figures, a rehearsed line delivered too smoothly)
  rather than in messy narration — the prose stays composed even when she
  isn't, which is the point.
- **Jack (retired detective):** procedural, observational rhythm — clauses
  that catalog details the way a report would (time of day, who was where),
  a habit left over from decades of work. Notices what's out of place before
  anything else. Dry, understated interior commentary; avoids sentimentality
  even about his own grief, which should read as restraint rather than
  coldness.
- **Olivia (single mother):** economical, practical sentences organized
  around tasks and time ("Twenty minutes until the bus. Soup first, then the
  bills."); very little indulgent description because she doesn't have the
  bandwidth for it — her attention is triaged. Notices cost, time,
  and what her son needs before her own needs register at all.
- **Liam (teenager):** more sensory and present-tense-feeling even in past
  tense narration; run-on or fragmented sentences reflecting restlessness;
  vocabulary skews contemporary/informal, may reference music or lyrics he's
  writing. Notices sound and social hierarchy (who's watching, who's
  judging) more than the physical setting.

## Craft techniques to apply this consistently

- **Free indirect discourse**, not "she thought" tags — let the narration's
  own word choices and rhythm shift to match the POV character's mind
  without announcing it, so the register itself carries the character.
- **Filter the environment through the character's expertise/concerns.**
  The same street can be "good afternoon light" (Sarah), "two ways out"
  (Michael), or "that's the Hendersons' porch, third overdue notice this
  month" (Jack, if his old casework touches the town).
- **Write a one-line "voice test" per character** and check every new
  chapter's opening paragraph against it before drafting further — a quick
  gut check for whether the paragraph could be swapped into another
  character's chapter without anyone noticing (if so, the voice isn't
  differentiated yet).
- **Avoid a shared stock of description.** The current draft reuses "deep
  blue eyes," "dark eyes," "facade" across multiple characters — retire
  physical-description shorthand entirely in favor of the
  attention-filtering technique above; it does more characterizing work
  than eye color ever will.
