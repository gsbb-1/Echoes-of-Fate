# Sarah

- **Role:** young artist
- **Wants:** to break free from her past and her family's expectations
- **Obstacle:** memories of a past she longed to escape; feels confined by
  what her family expects of her
- **Backstory:** Sarah Bennett grew up in Willow Creek, the only child of Tom
  and Diane Bennett, who have run Bennett's Hardware on Main Street for three
  generations. She was six years old the autumn Daniel Voss disappeared —
  her family kept a small fishing cabin near the lake/orchard, sold not long
  after, and she was there, underfoot, the week it happened. She has no
  clear memory of that week; what surfaces instead, sometimes at the edge of
  sleep, is a fragment with no context — cold water, raised adult voices
  somewhere she couldn't see, being carried. She's never told anyone this,
  because she's never had words for it and because it's always seemed too
  thin to be a real memory rather than something she invented. When the
  lake/orchard turns up in her painting without her meaning to put it there,
  she tells herself it's just a motif she's fixated on. Her parents expected
  her to take over the store, or at least "do something sensible" — she went
  two years into a business degree at her mother's insistence before
  dropping out to paint full-time, which her mother still calls "the year
  Sarah quit." She rents a studio apartment above the old feed store: close
  enough to walk to her parents' shop, far enough that she doesn't have to.
  *(Resolved by plot-research, 2026-09-14 — see `scenes/plot-map.md`: the
  cabin was Hollis family land — Diane's maiden name — sold to Warren Hale
  the same autumn Daniel disappeared under financial hardship, the same
  transaction that put the family there that week. Sarah's fragment is
  real but is very likely her memory of the sale's tension, not of
  Daniel's death, which she had no way to witness.)*
- **Voice / persona:** Runs long and associative when she's absorbed in
  work, cataloguing light and color in clauses that pile on each other, then
  snaps into short fragments the instant she's interrupted or made to
  perform normalcy for someone (a customer, her mother on the phone).
  Diction is unpretentious and tactile — no gallery-essay vocabulary, just
  the working language of a studio (underpainting, going muddy, a line that
  "won't commit"), because she came to painting through doing it, not
  studying it. What she notices first in any space: light and its direction
  and temperature, then texture and surface; faces and words register last,
  which is part of why she can go through a whole conversation not quite
  hearing what was said. Metaphor source: paint, canvas, and studio
  vocabulary — mixing, underpainting, negative space. Voice test: "The light
  was doing that thing again, flattening everything gold, and she was three
  brushes deep before she noticed her phone was ringing."
- **Physical description:** A thin white scar across the heel of her left
  palm, from a childhood fall she has no specific memory of — only that it
  happened "near water." Paint permanently ghosted into the cuticle of her
  right thumb, visible in every photo of her since she was about twenty.
- **Internal want vs. external want:** Externally, she wants to sell her
  work under her own name and build a reputation in Willow Creek that has
  nothing to do with the store or her mother's approval. Internally, she
  needs to find out why the lake/orchard image won't leave her alone — and,
  underneath that, permission to trust a memory that isn't fully hers to
  trust before her family talks her out of it.
- **Flaw:** Conflict-avoidant to the point of self-erasure — she'd rather
  absorb a feeling into a painting than say it out loud to the person it's
  about. It keeps her safe and keeps her stuck: she still hasn't opened her
  mother's voicemails, still hasn't asked her parents a direct question
  about the cabin they sold, and lets Michael's questions go further than
  her own.
- **Introduced:** Chapter 1, painting alone in her studio apartment at dawn
- **Arc** (chapter numbers per `manuscript/outline.md`'s 26-chapter table):
  - Ch. 1 — introduced, alone, painting
  - Ch. 3 — notices Michael around town before they speak; her current
    painting nags at her
  - Ch. 4 — meets Michael properly
  - Ch. 12 — their paths keep crossing; coincidence starts to feel like
    something else
  - Ch. 15 — relationship deepens; Michael's real reason for being in
    town starts to leak through
  - Ch. 17 — learns what her recurring painted image actually is
  - Ch. 25-26 — reckons with a family history she was never told; her arc
    resolves alongside Michael's
- **Relationships:** Michael (love interest / catalyst)
- **Plot thread (resolved 2026-09-14):** Sarah has been painting a
  recurring, unexplained image of a lake/orchard she has no conscious
  memory of visiting. It's the place Daniel was last seen. Her mother's
  family, the Hollises, owned that land and sold it to Warren Hale the
  same autumn Daniel disappeared — the same week the six-year-old
  fragment (see Backstory) comes from. Full detail: `scenes/plot-map.md`.
- **Key relationships (beyond Michael):**
  - **Emma:** Bennett's Hardware carries a line of credit through Hale
    Development, and Sarah's mother speaks of Emma's late father, Warren
    Hale, with the same reverence she uses for the bank. Sarah finds Emma's
    polish faintly intimidating and has never had a real conversation with
    her.
  - **Jack:** He's known her since she was small enough to be underfoot in
    her parents' shop and still calls her "kiddo." He was on the ground the
    week her family's cabin situation touched the edge of his case notes,
    though neither of them has ever said so aloud.
  - **Olivia:** Close in age; Olivia babysat her a handful of times as a
    young teenager. They're friendly the way people who share a hairdresser
    are friendly, not close.
  - **Liam:** He's seen her paintings in the café window downtown and
    thinks the recurring lake image is "kind of metal" without knowing why
    it unsettles him — he's never said so to her.
