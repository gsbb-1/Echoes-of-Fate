Chapter 3: The Artist and the Stranger

It was two days since Owen had driven off with the three-cafés piece wrapped in brown paper, and one day since she'd told herself, kneeling in the loft above the feed store, that she was going to go ask him about the back wall. The asking had taken a while. She woke early, the way the building had its own dawn, and lay on the couch in her coat for half an hour with the flat stack of canvases propped across the room, rehearsing what she'd say and then what the point of saying it was and then whether the point mattered enough to risk the version of her this town kept filed under the hardware store's daughter who paints if she asked and it went badly.

She went anyway. That was usually the difference — she went anyway. The empty sleeve of last night's jacket hung off the arm of the couch, and she took the portfolio case in her good hand and went down the back stairs, out into a street that didn't have a verdict on her yet this early.

Sarah had the question ready three different ways by the time she got to the café, which was usually a sign she wasn't actually going to ask it. The first version was direct — I want a show, my own work, not the Main Street stuff — clean and terrifying. The second hedged it into a question about wall space, whether Owen ever rotated the back corner past the espresso machine for anything besides the community-board flyers. The third, which she suspected was the one she'd actually use, buried the ask so far inside a joke that Owen could let her joke her way out of it if he wanted to, and she could let him.

She stood outside a minute longer than she needed to, portfolio case against her leg, watching her own reflection layer itself over the window display — the three-cafés piece from Tuesday propped in its usual spot now, the paint barely dry, competent and sunlit and entirely safe.

She'd told herself on the way that she wasn't opening the case until Owen had actually answered, because an ask that took three mornings deserved at least the dignity of being asked for. The case held two canvas flats, the smallest she had — a night piece she'd meant to pitch to the dentist's waiting room and a still life she'd stopped believing in halfway through. Less chosen than portable, both of them, and she knew it. The thing she was actually carrying had to do with the corner Owen had already seen, the one in the window piece, scraped back and repainted twice since Tuesday and still there under the good gold paint, the way you could feel a bruise under skin that looked fine.

That was when she noticed him. Across the street, half in the shade of the bank awning, a man she didn't recognize, doing the thing she'd caught someone doing outside this same window three days ago and had told herself she'd imagined — not window-shopping, not waiting for anyone, just looking, at the painting, in a way that had nothing casual in it. He wasn't looking at her. She was fairly sure of that. It was the painting he kept coming back to, his attention sliding off it toward the street and then dragging back, the way her own eye kept sliding back to the corner she'd repainted.

Old jacket, wrong for the weather, zipped to the sternum when the day called for shirtsleeves. Dark hair, and under the incidental details a way of standing she recognized from a summer spent painting figures from photographs — weight forward, hands loose, ready to move before he knew he was moving — and his attention kept skipping to the street and the parking lot and the bank door, the exits, the way a man's attention skipped when the looking itself was the thing he was nervous about. Not shopping. Shopping had a pace to it, a set of weights and measures. This was study, from a dark spot under an awning, at a painting of three cafés by a painter nobody had ever studied, and it put a crick in her day the way a half-remembered face did in the middle of the night.

She had, for one held breath, the impulse to cross the street and ask him what, precisely, was so interesting about a picture of three cafés. She didn't. She had a portfolio case and a rehearsed question and just the amount of nerve required for one confrontation this morning, and she'd already spent most of it getting this far.

Owen was restocking the pastry case when the bell over the door announced her, and he straightened up with the particular relief of a man who'd been hoping for a customer and gotten someone he didn't have to perform for instead.

"Tell me that's not another one," he said, nodding at the case.

"It's not. I mean — it's not for sale, I mean, it's not commissioned." She set the case down against the counter instead of opening it, which wasn't the plan, the plan had involved actually showing him something. "I wanted to ask you about the back wall."

"The flyer wall."

"You said once — I might be misremembering — that you'd thought about rotating it. Local art, not just, you know." She gestured, vaguely, at the window, at the three cafés, at every piece of hers currently hanging in this town that looked like a postcard instead of like something she'd made. "Not that. Something else."

Owen wiped his hands on his apron and looked at her for a second too long, not unkindly, the way people looked at you when they could tell you'd walked in with more to say than you were saying. "You mean your stuff. The real stuff."

"I mean whatever I bring you," she said, which was a retreat, and she heard it be one as it left her mouth.

"Bring me something," Owen said. "Not the water thing you keep doing in the corners of everything — something whole. I'll hang it. I've got room after the festival banners come down." He said it easily, like it cost him nothing, which it probably didn't, and which made it worse somehow, that the door she'd spent three separate mornings rehearsing her way through had just swung open because she'd finally, badly, half-asked.

Her hands were on the case latches before she'd decided to open it, and she caught herself, the way you caught yourself before a curb you hadn't looked at. "Not ready," she said.

Owen didn't push, which was worse than pushing. He wiped the counter once, slowly, looking at the case the way you'd look at a stray that might let you near it if you didn't spook it. "You bring me the ones you're proud of," he said. "Those mostly hang themselves. Bring me one you're still nervous about — that's the one the wall wants."

The bell went, and a woman came in for a black coffee and a scone, and Owen turned into the easy version of himself, the one that cost him nothing, and Sarah stood at the counter with the case latches under her thumbs and listened to her mother, who was never deep under the surface — something sensible, the degree, the store — a sentence six years old that had never once been the thing her own work was for.

When the woman left, Owen said, as if he'd been saving it: "Wall's empty in ten days, once the festival's packed up. Save me from the flyers, and I'll save you the corner with the good light."

She couldn't find a joke to stand behind, so she said the whole thing plainly, which was the first time she'd ever said it out loud in a way that didn't leave him room to let her off: "Okay. I'll bring you something whole."

"That's the deal," Owen said.

She bought a coffee she didn't especially want, mostly to have something to do with her hands on the way out, and when she came back through the door the man across the street was gone — not walking away, just gone, the way someone was gone when you'd stopped watching just long enough to miss their leaving. She stood on the sidewalk with her coffee going cold in her hand and looked at the space under the bank awning where he'd been, and then at her own painting in the window, and felt, for no reason she could have defended out loud, that the two absences were somehow the same shape.

She didn't have room for that today either. She had, for the first time in more than a year, a wall with good light on it and a deadline she'd set herself, and the whole walk home she made herself list, out loud under her breath like a woman talking herself down off a ledge, every subject that wasn't water.

The courthouse clock after dark, the light pooling in the second-floor windows and going nowhere. Her father's hands, patient over a brass hinge at his shop counter, patient the way he was with everything except his own name. The bent stop sign at the cross street where someone had taken the corner too fast last spring and the town had never gotten around to straightening it. The east light, the twenty minutes of it, the gold that wanted nothing from her.

She'd learned a long time ago that the surest way to keep an image from ambushing a painting was to paint it on purpose, out where she could face it. She'd done that with water for three years now, and the water had kept coming anyway — six or seven inches of a place she'd never been, dark and patient, every time her attention slipped. Maybe the trick wasn't new subjects. Maybe the corner Owen was saving her, the one with the best light, was the very thing she'd been painting out of the corner of her eye since she could hold a brush, and the reason she'd never painted it full-size, facing her, was that she didn't know what it was yet, or what it would ask of her to finally meet it.

There was a version of this where she tested the theory. There was a version where she didn't, and kept it safe, and kept calling it what she'd always called it: negative space. A technical choice.

The man under the bank awning had not looked at it like a technical choice.

So she stood at the bottom of the feed-store stairs for a while, the case going heavy in her hand, and did not go up, because going up meant deciding between the two versions — the safe paint and the whole thing — and she could feel, rising where the nerve ordinarily went, that she was about to be done with safe paint.

She could not, for the life of her, have said what she was waiting on.
