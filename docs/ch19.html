Chapter 19: Webs

The coffee truck was on time and Ruth's coffee was poured before the register, and the diner was doing what the diner did on a morning with a headline in it: making the same sentence over and over until it came out smooth as a pot handle. Human remains. The old orchard. "They think it's that Voss boy, is what I hear." A line of men at the counter turning the words over and setting them down warmer each time, and Jack Muldoon sipped his coffee at his usual stool and watched the web the way he'd watched every web, cataloguing the strands: the parcel, settled and silent again now that the county had sealed it off; the name Hale hanging in the air of the room the way it didn't usually hang; the girl at the end of the counter wiping the same stretch of counter with her back to the room, and the boy who'd handed the town a postcard out at the motel off the county road, and the painter's new piece up at the café drawing people past the window who'd have walked on by a week ago.

Eighteen years, and every strand led to the same place, and the only man left who'd been standing on that ground the night it happened had gone to ground over the county line the way he'd done eighteen years ago, days after the search went out. Jack set the coffee down and put a dollar on the counter. Today was going to be the question he'd been saving since the autumn, and before the old foreman heard about the discovery from the town and built himself a wall out of the rumor, Jack wanted to be standing on the far side of his door first.

Sarah found Michael where she'd told him she'd be, in front of the hung piece at the café, with his hands at his sides and the morning light coming through the window onto the ground he was holding in paint. She stood beside him a moment, letting him have the silence he kept, and then she said, quiet, the way you hand over something heavy without dropping it, "That ground was ours, Michael. My family's. The whole of it — the orchard, the lake frontage, the cabin up the track. Hollis ground, my mother's people, back to before the town remembers. And the autumn Danny was here, they sold it. All of it, to the Hale company. The same week." She watched his face and made herself keep going, the way she'd made herself stand in front of the finished thing and not run. "My mother packed me out of that cabin in her arms. That's what I've been painting — the ground we lost. The week the town lost your brother, my family lost the ground he was standing on."

Michael looked at the painting a long while, and the exactness went out of his voice and left it plain, the way it had gone plain in front of this canvas before. "The Hales bought it that week."

"The same week." She said it like a confession, because it had started to feel like one — as though her family's leaving had something to do with his family's dying, which she knew in her bones it didn't, and which she couldn't shake the weight of anyway. "Before the search even went out. Before anyone had a name for what happened out there."

He nodded once, a man taking the ground of a thing into his hands, and she saw him put it somewhere careful, not dropped, not yet spoken of — the way he handled the whole of his life, she was learning, by taking every piece of it into his own hands and deciding its weight. "That makes it hers in a way," he said, finally, quiet. "The ground under the picture. It belonged to your family before it belonged to his death." He looked at her then, and the look was the plain one, the one with no door in it. "Thank you for telling me. That's the whole of the ground, now. You gave me the last piece of it."

Sarah stood in front of the painting with the man who'd turned her whole life's work into someone's graveside, and she held his silence with him, and the café opened its morning around them, and neither of them moved for a long time.

Jack crossed the county line by eleven, the road climbing out of the valley and coming down the far side into the flats, the way it had climbed the afternoon, days ago, when he'd come this way and stood in the yard and not knocked. He was knocking now. The place was a one-side-gabled house with a dead tractor in the side yard and a porch holding a man's worth of old chairs, and Hank Colby came to the door in the way of a man who'd seen the truck coming and used the time to decide whether to answer.

He was old — older than the years in the file, which was the way of years in this valley. The knees the town line said had driven him back over the county line were gone, and he stood hunched in the doorway with his hands hanging, and he looked at Jack Muldoon the way a man looks at the one bill he'd mailed back to the courthouse for eighteen years.

"Muldoon."

"Hank." Jack stood on the porch in his old-man coat, cataloguing what was out of place, which was that Colby hadn't asked him why. "They found something out at the orchard this morning."

Colby didn't move. "What something."

"Human remains. In the north bank, where the regrade went in the autumn of 2008." Jack let that sit. "The one I never got a work order for. The one that wasn't in the county permit file — I checked, Hank. It's not in any file. It's just in the ground, with the boy who went walking into it one night and never walked out."

The silence between them did the thing porch silences do with a valley filling it up. Then Colby said, "You come to put words in it, Muldoon."

"I come to hear them. I'm retired. I got a man out there at the motel who's waited eighteen years to hear what I hear, and I'm not going to hand him a story I haven't stood behind first." Jack put his weight on the porch board. "You were the foreman. You were out there the night that boy went walking into the ground. He found something out there that Hale wasn't going to like, and you're the only man left alive who knows what he found."

Colby stood a long moment with his hands hanging, and the wind came down off the far ridge and moved through the dead tractor, and then he did what a man does at the end of a held breath: he let it out. "He found the inspection," he said. "The safety report on the old pond bank. Hale got it from the county inspector and never filed it, and told me to keep clearing around it, and the kid — he found it in the trailer, the week before." Colby looked down at his own hands as if they'd kept the memory for him. "Said if we didn't stop work and fix that bank, he was taking it to the county himself. Said it to my face, in the yard, loud enough for the whole crew to hear. I went out to the pond that night to settle it — nobody on that ground with a boy yelling about a report they all knew was there."

It came out in pieces, the way a man's confession comes out when he's said it to himself in the dark for eighteen years. "It was dark. Moon off the water. We argued, and I saw it go out of him, the argument, and then the bank —" He made a sound that might have been a laugh and wasn't. "The bank gave. It just gave, under both of us. He went down into the water and he didn't come up. I went in after him." He stopped. "I went in after him, Muldoon. It was black water and mud and he didn't come up, and I stood in it till my legs gave out and then I didn't know what to do, so I did what I did my whole life — I called the boss."

Jack stood very still on the porch, holding the shape of it in his hands the way he'd held the shape of everything: an accident. Not a killing. A bankful of unsound ground and a boy who'd found the paper that said so — exactly the paper Warren Hale never filed. "Hale came out that night."

"Hale came out with his truck and his men and a machine moved in from the south yard under a week's worth of 'erosion control' talk, and they put that bank back the way it never was, and the boy with it." Colby looked up at last, and the old man's eyes were ruined in the way of a man who'd watched something he couldn't unhappen. "He paid me like a layoff — severance, they called it, wrote me a check and a paper I signed — and I left town before the week was out, because if I stayed one more day I was going to tell somebody, and telling somebody was the only thing I had left to sell." He let the rest go out of him, a long breath, the whole of the used-up middle of his life. "I've been saying it in the dark for eighteen years. It's good to say it in the light."

Jack stood on the porch with the account of it settled into the shape he'd been carving for eighteen years — the accident, the panic, the call, the machine, the money, the leaving — every strand of the web answering to itself at last, none of it in the county's record yet, all of it weighted with the only word a retired detective's push carried. "You'll tell them that," he said. "The county's. You'll tell them the whole of it, the way you told me."

"I'll tell them." Colby said it without a fight, the way a man says the end of a long debt. "They found him. The least I can do is say what he found."

Jack walked back down the porch stairs and crossed the yard with the dead tractor in the side, and he didn't look back, because he'd learned, in thirty-one years, what a man's back looked like when he'd given up the last thing he was carrying.

He drove back over the ridge and took the long road past the lake and stood in the motel doorway while Michael Voss read it out of his face before he'd said a word, and then he told him the whole of it, the way Colby had told him — the inspection, the argument, the bank giving way in the dark, the call, the truck, the machine that had put his brother back into the ground under a company's name. He left nothing out except his own weight on it, which was a man's business to carry himself.

Michael sat on the edge of the bed with his hands flat on his knees and took it the way he'd taken everything this month — in pieces he decided the weight of — and when Jack finished, he said, quiet, "An accident."

"An accident," Jack said. "And a cover-up after it. A company protecting itself. That's the whole shape of it, as far as a man can say." He paused. "There's more, Michael." And he told him about Sarah, and the Hollis ground, and the sale the same week the ground let his brother go — that the very parcel Hale had buried his boy on had been bought that autumn from the family of the woman who painted it.

Michael put his face in his hands a moment, and when he looked up the grief was the plain kind, the first Jack had seen on him. "The man's dead. Warren Hale — he's dead. Who's left to answer for it." The question sat between them a long moment. "His daughter runs it. Does she know?"

"I think she knows something," Jack said, and he heard his own voice go careful, the way it went careful when his hands had gone cold. "I sat in her office and asked her about her father's old ground, and she knew, Michael — knew the shape of it, the way you know a door you've been standing in front of. What she knows, or how much, or what she's holding — that's the piece of the web I haven't reached yet."

They sat with the motel light on and the town settling below, and the weight of the whole web hung between them — the accident, the cover-up, the bought ground, the woman who ran the company that buried his brother and the boy she'd grown up beside, none of it official, all of it close enough to touch. Michael studied the quiet shape of the motel room wall a long while, and then he said, the way a man says a thing he's laying down for the first time: "Then I'll go ask her daughter myself. The way I should have asked her the first day."
