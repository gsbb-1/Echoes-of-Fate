Chapter 11: Six Crossings

The man at the front desk was asking about the parcel, and Priya had done the math on him before Emma heard the first of it: a stranger, no sign-off, asking after the one decade that had been bothering her for a week. The same decade a foreman's records simply stopped.

"He says research. Family history. Wants the survey and boundary records from before the company went digital." Priya had his name on a Post-it, turned against her palm so the man couldn't see it being handled like it mattered. "Michael Voss. I told him the historicals live at the county, but he asked for someone who'd actually know the parcel."

The name went through Emma like a number that didn't belong in a column, one that threw the whole ledger out. Voss. She'd lived with the folder for a year and a half and the folder had somehow never come with a family attached — because a folder with no victim in it was a folder a woman could keep. Here was the name that would have been the victim's, waiting politely in her lobby for old records. She invited him in. What else did a building like hers do with a civil question?

"Mr. Voss. I'm Emma Hale. Priya tells me you're interested in the lake parcel."

"My family had an interest in that land once." Everything about the sentence was rehearsed, and she'd have bet the rehearsal on the drive over. "I hoped you'd have the old boundary records, so I could see where the lines ran."

"We don't hold originals from before the digitization. The county recorder's office has the full survey chain." She caught her right thumbnail against her back teeth and set her hand flat on the desk, which he saw. "I can have Priya assemble the current chain of title. It's boring, but it's complete."

"And you'd know if there was anything unusual about it. You've run this ground eighteen years."

"Eighteen years of carrying costs," she said dry.

"If I find it's the ground I think it is," he said, "I'll be back with better questions than survey lines."

"I'm sure you will."

He was almost to the glass wall when he turned, a deliberate turn, not a crack in the exit. "You never asked what I wanted it for."

"I assumed you'd tell me," she said, "if it mattered."

He held her look a beat longer than the sentence required and went. Priya, from the hallway, gave the door a long look of her own, and Emma watched her sock the two of them away — too careful at each other, in a room full of ordinary business — because Priya had cleaned up files that old this week, and she had a memory like a clean ledger.

Alone, Emma stood at the glass, where she stood when she needed to see the town without being in it, and made the first appointment she'd ever made with the folder. She did not tell herself not yet. She'd retired that word the night of the groundbreaking, standing on the seam of backfilled ground where grass had never taken; she had understood, standing there, that it was a way of pretending a decision wasn't already made. What she decided now, watching a Voss walk down the block with the light behind him, was narrower and more honest: when the asking got close enough to find the folder on its own, she would be the one who moved it first. She would carry the name she'd built out to that ground herself. It was not a good plan. It was, for the first time, a plan she'd chosen.

Out on the street, Michael Voss walked the two blocks to his truck cataloguing exits, the way he catalogued every new town — the alley, the COUNTY RECORDING sign hanging crooked on the annex — and told himself the meeting had gone the way meetings with people who knew things went: the knowledge thanked you for your time and gave you nothing. But she had never once asked why a man with no accent for miles wanted the history of a parcel she'd sat on eighteen years. A clerk with clean hands would have asked. It was the smallest tell he'd ever gotten, and enough, and it was the whole lesson of eighteen years of short-haul jobs: you don't need a door to open to know which one has something behind it.

He climbed into the truck and did a thing he hadn't done since he was nineteen — thought of a town in the plural. He'd come to find a place. He'd found, instead, a ground, a case that had opened again, and a woman who knew something, and he was not going to be done with any of it by the end of the week. He sat with his hands on the wheel and let the discomfort in — the specific weight of a man who had just committed to a place without deciding to — and drove to the county annex, because county records kept hours and the ones he wanted counted on his being patient.

The festival was six weeks out and the town was already treating a date on a calendar like a deadline agreed to collectively, at different paces. Ruth brought it into the kitchen over the morning rush: the committee wanted cleanup crews through festival week, real hours, holiday pay. The math appeared in Olivia's head complete and self-loading the way it always did — diner days plus four nights plus festival week came out to almost exactly what Friday needed — and she said yes before her stomach had finished objecting. The objection had a shape she didn't name, the way she never named shapes: festival week was the closest the town got to that end of the valley, the crowds and stalls trailing back past the parcel's tree line, and the historical society had its anniversary exhibit running, which she'd been told twice now by the one person in town whose digging she couldn't stop, because he was her son.

She took the hours anyway. That was the whole of it. She took them the way she took everything, head down, the math first, and folded the shape into the drawer with the rest of the unsaid things.

Sarah Bennett came in mid-morning and took the corner booth with a sketchbook open, not drawing in it, the way she'd been in that booth on and off for weeks, looking at things the way she'd look at one before she painted it. Olivia filled her coffee, and Sarah, thinking out loud to the hands doing a needed thing, said she'd gotten a real wall — Owen's back wall at the café — and had to bring him something whole, and the problem was she kept painting the same place, a place she'd decided didn't exist, and lately she'd been wondering if it might be out past the lake, and did Olivia remember that land at all, the old orchard ground, what was left of it?

Olivia's hands were holding the creamers, and she set them down flat for a beat — the old tell, retired often, never fully — and the truth was available, composed and ready: my dad ran crew out there that season, we went where the work was, that ground's been Hale's for years. A true sentence, none of it a lie. "That's a different town's worth of years," she said. "Before my time, mostly." And carried the coffee pot on, because the floor wasn't going to fill itself, and Sarah let it go the way she let all things go, the ease of a person who'd learned young that the direct question got you the flat no.

Sarah paid and walked out, and you could have tracked her through the two blocks of Main and out the county road like a single unhurried line. She stopped at the chained gate where the NO TRESPASSING sign had gone silver with age, on the exact spot a man in a truck with a cracked watch had stopped three weeks ago without getting out — neither of them ever needing to know they'd stood on the same ground. Down the track the equipment had been at work; she could hear it over the treeline, a steady rhythm where the orchard used to be. The treeline arching down off the ridge, the low ground, the long pale shine at the far end that had to be the lake: it all resolved the way a shape resolves when you finally look at it from the right angle. This was it. This was the place she'd been painting with her eyes closed for three years, in the margins of everything, the place she'd told herself was nowhere because nowhere was easier than the alternative.

Underneath it sat a thinner strangeness — that some version of her had stood here before, small, and been carried away from a sound like water. She dropped it the way she dropped it always, into the place she kept the things too thin to be memory. But she tore a page out of the sketchbook on the walk home and made the first quick study of the actual place, and decided she was coming back with a canvas. Nothing she'd ever made had looked at her the way this ground was starting to.

The archive closed at eight, and Jack had been the last one in it for forty minutes, which suited him, and Liam was the second-to-last, which did not, though he'd have slit his own throat before admitting it. Jack had caught the boy at the reader three times in two weeks, each time with a different reason and the same look, and had let him stay because he recognized the look: nothing in this town had looked at him like that in years, and he'd been younger than Liam the last time his had.

Tonight Jack was going back through the same week's coverage for the third time that month — the front page of the Valley Weekly from the week of the disappearance, the photograph the boy had logged into the exhibit folder, the same image that had been chasing Liam for weeks.

"That one's yours," Jack said, not a question.

"I put it in the exhibit folder. Mrs. Okafor said it deserved to be worked properly." Liam had it held up to the light, crooked, the way you hold a thing that hasn't finished saying itself. "It's the composition. The line across the rows is basically a chord. If I could find the note it's —" He stopped, caught being fifteen. "Why do you keep coming back to it?"

"Because the line's the whole story. That many people walking the same ground, looking for the one place on it that's different from all the others. Somebody walks until some part of him notices the ground that doesn't match." Jack said the rest to himself: the graded bank at the north edge of the pond, the seam of grass that had never taken, the one patch that had changed while the town walked it.

Liam looked at the photograph a moment, then, smaller, the voice doing the actual searching: "My mom grew up here. She doesn't say anything about that ground. Ever."

Jack heard the sentence for what it cost the boy saying it and did not reach for the shape of it — the girl in the file, three interviews, one statement of record, the flat voice saying nothing, nobody, no in the particular way of a kid who'd come early to her own protection. He'd promised himself, the night he'd been led back to the box by a postcard, he was done reaching for that thread. He let the silence sit. "When I retired I told myself I was done with that ground. Spent six years believing it in the daylight. Walking it again's the only honest part of me left." He set the photograph flat on the reader. "I'm going to go have a look at it before it stops being ground. That's what I keep coming back here to practice for."

"Can I come?"

"Not yet." He said it like a man holding a door, not to keep the boy out but to keep the room behind it from getting the story going before either of them understood it. "Looking's the job. You keep looking."

He walked home slower than the doctor liked, knee talking about weather that was still a day out.

Behind him, Liam took the long way home along the county road and stopped once at the chained gate where a woman with a sketchbook had stood that morning, looked down the track at the equipment blinking its work-lights in the dusk, and decided he'd find the other face — the one at the edge of his mother's photograph, half cut out of the frame, the man who'd been standing on that ground when it was still a place anybody could stand on. Somebody in this town was from that summer. Somebody still had the years.

He stepped through his own front door, watching his mother cross the kitchen with her ringer's economy, and understood, without being able to say it, that a town isn't as big as it likes to think. One burned-out kitchen light, one county road, one photograph propped against another. You keep looking long enough, the ground does the rest.
