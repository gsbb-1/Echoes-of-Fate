Chapter 10: The Notebook

The scanner had a rhythm he'd started hearing as a beat, hum-click-clunk, hum-click-clunk, four folders to a bar, and Liam had settled into it the way you settle into a song you don't love — half in, half not, an ear open for the part that might surprise you. Box forty-one was long done. He was into the fifties now, the exhibit folders, and the work had gotten dull and then interesting and then dull again, which was how he seemed to do everything lately, in waves that crested and went flat before he could ride either one.

He didn't mean to pull up the picture. Mrs. Okafor — always Mrs., never first name, a small-town rule he respected the way he respected most small-town rules, by half-running them — had told him to log it if he moved it, weeks ago, and he hadn't logged it, he'd photographed it with his phone. The photo was still on his phone: a scan of a scan, grain gray as rain, a line of volunteers walking shoulder to shoulder through a bare orchard, heads down, the search line dragging its fence across the rows. It was in his camera roll between a blurry ceiling and a half of his own hand, which was where all his discoveries lived, ungroomed.

He zoomed it out until the whole frame fit. He'd stared at this photo enough times in two weeks that he'd stopped pretending there was a school-project reason and started just looking, the way you look at a sample of something you might want to use later. Something about the composition kept pulling at him — the line of the treeline along the top, the low ground, the sliver of light at the far end that could have been water — a shape inside the picture that was almost a chord, that wanted resolving, that he couldn't find the next note for.

"Mrs. Okafor?"

She appeared in the doorway of the workroom the way she always appeared, without having walked there. She looked at the phone in his hand like she already knew.

"Found something good?"

"Old one. Missing guy." He turned the screen to her, the way you'd hold up a found thing. "Search through the orchard, whatever year this was. I keep —" He didn't finish; finishing it would have meant naming the itch, and he didn't have a name for it, he had a feeling that wouldn't scan.

"Voss," Mrs. Okafor said, and something in the room got softer — not sad, gone smooth from use, the way a word got smooth when the whole town had said it for years and then mostly stopped. "I remember that one. I was a girl." She looked at the photo a second, no more, and handed the phone back. "You log that one properly if you're going to hang onto it. It's exhibit material; it deserves the folder. That's the rule, not a suggestion."

"Yes, ma'am." And because the room had gone a little soft, he asked the question that had been sitting in him for two weeks without a reason: "What happened to him? Like, actually? The guy just — gone?"

Mrs. Okafor looked at him for a beat, weighing how much of the town's old story to hand a boy in a workroom, and then she said, careful, the way someone would say a thing they'd been careful with: "He never came home from this town. Nobody ever got an answer that satisfied anybody." She paused. "There's an old fellow who'll know more than I do, if you're that curious. Took the case back when. He still comes in here, every so often, looking at the same papers."

The old fellow turned out to be sitting at the microfilm reader in the corner of the reading room, and Liam almost didn't clock him as the cop until he looked up — late sixties, rope-callused hands lying flat on the steel like he was holding the surface still, and the kind of quiet that meant he'd been in this room before the microfilm was. He looked at Liam the way Liam's mother looked at a stranger at the door: cataloging, not alarmed.

"You're Olivia's boy," the old man said. "Jack Muldoon."

"Liam. I'm, uh —" He held up the phone, which felt stupid, but it was what he had. "I'm doing the archive digitizing. Community service." He said it the way you say a thing that usually needs a defense, and then found he didn't want to defend it to this man, which was odd.

"Let me see," Jack Muldoon said, and Liam was across the room before he'd decided to cross it, phone out, the photograph filling the frame, and the old man looked at it a long quiet while in the stale archive light.

"Eighteen years," Jack said, and there was something in his voice like a door being held open a crack. "Three weeks of lines through that orchard. I walked that ground every day for two of them." He looked up at Liam — not at the phone, at him. "You finding this on your own?"

"Just scanning folders."

"Uh-huh." The old man's eyes went to the screen again, and something in his face moved behind its own stillness — the look of a man watching a younger version of somebody he once was. "You keep looking," he said. "You look long enough, you'll find something. That's the whole job. Most people just don't look long enough." And he turned back to the microfilm, which was, Liam realized, as close to a blessing as this town gave.

He logged the photo properly, the way Mrs. Okafor had said, in the working folder for the exhibit, and then he walked home with his hands in his pockets and the itch in his chest doing double time, because the old man had looked at him and not laughed, and nobody looked at him and not laughed once they'd had a look at him.

His mother was working tonight — the offices, the building downtown, the ones that cleaned her out early and paid her late — which meant he had the apartment from late afternoon until nine-thirty, which meant the closet, which meant the box, and he told himself, all the way up the stairs, that he was looking for something else entirely, which was true, and that he wasn't going to get pulled into the old missing-man thing again, which was already a lie.

The box was on the top shelf of the hall closet, behind the coats his mother never wore, one of those shoeboxes with the lid held on by a rubber band so old it had gone brittle. He hadn't gone in it in years. He'd half forgotten it existed, which was maybe the point of it. He'd come here tonight for one thing, one piece of paper, one photograph, one scrap that proved the man his mother never talked about had happened — his father, who phoned on birthdays about half the time and phoned nowhere in between. He'd decided, this year, that he was done negotiating with a voice on the phone, and if there was a face to put to it somewhere in this house, he was going to find it.

There was a lot of his mother small in the box: the corsage she'd have worn to a dance, of course. School photos. A folded yearbook page. Receipts and church cards and the kind of things a person keeps without deciding to — and then, toward the bottom, a photograph that stopped his hand.

It was summer in the photo, the way all old photos are summer. A girl — his mother, unmistakably, the same jaw, the same way of standing with her weight on one hip — seventeen or so, in a t-shirt, at the edge of a field of apple trees in full leaf, the treeline rising behind her, and at the very edge of the frame, half cut off by the corner, a young man in a work shirt, mostly out of the picture, the way some people were always mostly out of whatever picture they were in.

He looked at his mother's face — she was smiling, which she almost never did in real time, a real smile, unguarded, the kind he'd have had to rob a bank to get out of her now — and he flipped the photo over, looking for a name, a date, anything, and the back was blank except for a felt-tip note gone fuzzy: the orchard, before. No name.

The orchard.

He looked at the photo, and then he did the thing the itch had been telling him to do for two weeks without a reason: he unlocked his phone and pulled up the archive photograph, the search line, and set the two screens side by side, his mother's summer field against the volunteers' bare rows, and the treeline in the background of one slotted into the treeline in the background of the other like the missing measure of a song.

It was the same place.

He didn't know — could not have known — what that meant; all he knew was that he'd been staring at that orchard in the old paper for weeks and his mother had been standing in it before the old paper was even worrying about the man who disappeared, and that his mother had never once, in his whole life, mentioned an orchard, or a summer, or a man half cut out of a photograph who might have mattered to her.

He set the family photo down carefully, on top of the box lid, next to the phone, and he watched the two images side by side in the failing light for a long while, the way you'd watch two samples that had to match and almost did.

Then he got his notebook — the one held together with tape, full of half-songs — and he found a blank page at the back, and he wrote, in the small tight hand he saved for the lines he didn't want to lose:

ground under the picture. picture under the ground. she's standing in both, still.

He read it back. He didn't know what it meant. That was the whole thing about the good ones — you didn't know what they meant yet; you just knew there was something under them, and if you let it sit it would come up through the surface on its own, the way everything he was ever going to be was supposed to come up through the surface.

He thought about posting it — the two photos, side by side, "this town is wild, my mom used to hang out where the search went" — and got as far as opening the app before something made him stop, some instinct about not letting the room get a story going before he understood the story, which wasn't like him but felt right, felt held, like the old man's voice saying you keep looking.

He closed the app. He closed the notebook. He put the family photo back in the box, deep, and put the box back on the top shelf, and he sat on the edge of his bed with his untuned guitar in his lap and picked the same four bars he'd been working on for a week, trying to find the fifth one, and the fifth one wouldn't come, because he was holding the answer to it somewhere he couldn't reach yet, in a box on a shelf, under a photograph of his mother standing in ground he'd been staring at for weeks without knowing it was hers.
