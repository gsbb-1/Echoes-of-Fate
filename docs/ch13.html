Chapter 13: Digging Deeper

The county records annex smelled the way records annexes had smelled since before anyone in the state had invented digital storage — paper and dust and the particular cold of a room full of things no one was waiting to see — and the clerk behind the main counter had forgotten Jack twice in the course of the morning, which Jack took as the finest possible sign. Nobody watching you is the second-best weapon in a small town. The best one is the habit of the public record, which sits in its drawer exactly as it was filed, indifferent to who opens it or why.

He asked for the 2008 files the way he'd asked for thirty years, by name and year and register, as if the records were his by right, which in a town this size, if you'd once been the only detective in it, they almost were. The clerk came back with a steel box the color of a municipality and set it on the counter between them, and Jack opened the lid and went in.

The crew registration for Hale Development, 2008 season, was one page of a yellowed form: company name, owner's signature — Warren Hale's, a hand he recognized from a decade of interacting with the county over fences and permits and the occasional dead deer — and a list of seasonal registrations. The foreman's line, in the middle, read Colby, Hank.

Underneath it, from the second county file, was the thing Jack had come for: the parcel's grading history, which was the county's polite way of keeping the record of who had been given permission to move ground. He ran his thumb down the column of permits and approvals for the lake parcel across that autumn, all the way to the end, where the season's last entry should have sat, and there was nothing.

No grading permit had ever been issued for the north section of that parcel. Not in 2008. Not after. There was no line in the county record that corresponded to the week his own note said a grader had worked the pond's north bank under the banner of erosion control. He read the column twice, the way you read a page when you already know what it's going to say, and then he closed the box and stood there a moment with his hand flat on the steel lid, letting the size of it settle: a machine had moved a substantial weight of earth, within the county's own jurisdiction, while the county's own apparatus was walking that same ground every day, and not a single page in any of this room's boxes had been asked to witness it.

He was still standing there when the door opened and Michael Voss came in.

Michael stopped at the sight of him the way a man stops at a fork in a road he thought was his alone. "Muldoon."

"Voss." Jack didn't ask what he was doing here; a man doing what Michael was doing left a trail a mile wide, and Jack had been following narrower ones for thirty years. "You found the same drawer I did."

"The clerk says you've been in here three mornings in a row." Michael came up to the counter and set a folder of his own down, county survey pages, the parcel's chain of title with the county registrar's stamps going back to before the Hollises had owned it. "Hale Development told me the historicals live here. Which was true — she was polite about it, and it was the politeness that wasn't true." He looked at Jack steadily. "Emma Hale never once asked me what I wanted the records for. A clerk with clean hands would have asked."

Jack filed that alongside everything else he'd filed about the name Hale this month — the postcard word, the moving ground, the careful daughter of a careful man — and it settled in with a weight he recognized. "Clean hands ask. You got the tell, then. I got one of my own." He slid the steel box back across the counter. "That regrade I told you about. The county never issued a permit for it. The ground moved, the week your brother's search lines were walking it, and no one in this building was ever asked to witness it."

Michael Voss read the implication in the same breath he read the room — the retracted posture of a man doing small arithmetic on a large sum. "And the foreman," he said. "Colby. He's the one who told your operator it was an 'order from the office.'"

"He's the one," Jack said. He hadn't told Michael the foreman's name. It hadn't needed telling; both of them had arrived at the same drawer from the same two facts, the way two men with the same map arrive at the same crossing. "And that's the piece that doesn't fit. He's in the county's 2008 crew registration. He's the man who said the ground was to move. And there is not one more trace of him in any record this county keeps, in either of those boxes, in eighteen years."

Michael took that in, and Jack watched him hold onto the counter with the particular stillness of a man raising his own stakes without permission. "Was there a reason he'd have left?"

"There's always a reason," Jack said. "That doesn't mean it's the same reason everyone's hoping for. You want to be careful which question you go out the door asking. It's the fastest way to turn a couple of pointed questions into a town story with your name on it, and a town story is the one thing you can't take back from a case."

When he said it he saw the boy in the archive, the runner's look, the same hunger with a different engine in it, and he filed Michael Voss alongside Liam Marsh the way he filed everything — as a variety of the same compulsive, half-lit thing he'd spent a life recognizing. "Let me do the asking in town for now," he said. "I'm the one nobody has to be afraid of. That's the whole of my retirement, right there."

The unsettling started at the hardware store, where Jack had bought rope and a saw blade and half the ordinary parts of a working life for thirty years, and where Tom Bennett — who ran the counter and had known Jack since before Jack had a badge, and whose whole family Jack could still recite the measurements of — looked at him across the loose nail bins and didn't pick up his change so much as hold it.

"The man out at the annex says you've been in there all week, pulling everything on the old parcel," Tom said, not a question. "I thought you were done with all that, Muldoon."

"A person's never done with a case while it's still open," Jack said. "Just sometimes they're tired enough to say so."

Tom put Jack's change on the counter and left it there, a small act of withdrawal, the town's oldest reflex: the moment you wanted something for yourself, everyone in reach stopped handing you anything for free. "That ground's been closed eighteen years," Tom said. "The man who owned it is gone. The crew's gone. Let the ground be ground."

"I'm not moving the ground," Jack said, and paid in exact coins, the way you pay when a transaction has turned formal, and walked out with the saw blade he didn't need under his arm and the sense behind him of a door that had just been drawn closed to a crack.

The gas station attendant who fueled Jack's truck on Tuesdays was an old-timer named Benson who'd worked the seasons out past the lake when he was young, and Jack asked, at the pump, not because he was being clever but because it was the straightest line to what he needed, "You worked the Hale crew back then. Remember a foreman named Colby? Hank Colby?"

Benson's hand stopped halfway through wiping the windshield, the way hands stop themselves in a town where a question from the right quarter means the ground is about to shift under somebody. "Colby," he said. "Sure I remember Colby. Ran the north crew the year the orchard came down. Hard man, done the kind of work that wore him into the shape of it." He looked out past the pump at the drawn front of the cold autumn morning. "Left overnight, though, end of that season. Didn't come back for his tools, didn't come say nothing to nobody. Just — gone. We always figured he knew something, the way a man skips out clean like that the week a whole town's out looking for a missing man. Nobody ever said so out loud. Nobody had to."

That was the town line, Jack understood, the one that had been sitting on the bench outside the hardware store for eighteen years, waiting for whoever finally had the standing to ask it. He didn't push. He pumped his gas and let the silence say the rest, and Benson, done with the windshield, gave him the last piece with the careful neutrality of a man who'd learned which doors in this town it was safe to stand in front of: "Funny thing, though. He's come back. Retired, last couple years, lives out past the county line the far side of the valley. Came back when his knees gave out, I heard. Man can't stay gone from the one place he belongs to, I guess."

Jack drove away from the pump with the address Benson had given him in his head, and the whole shape of the reopened file was suddenly not a before-and-after question but a present-tense one. The foreman who said order from the office had come home. The man who'd stood within reach of the only moving ground in the county the week Danny Voss vanished had been living, these last few years, across one ridge of the very valley his name had vanished out of.

He drove the length of the county line road without deciding to drive it, the way you go to the fridge you know is empty, and he got out at the end of a dirt track where a low house sat at the base of the far ridge, a shed with coats curled over a porch rail, a truck with the winter not quite off the tires. He stood outside it in the cold autumn wind with his hands in his pockets, the way he'd stood outside a hundred doors in his working life, and did not knock. There was a version of the afternoon in which he knocked on that door and the whole of it opened at once, and a version in which he did everything wrong and never got a second chance at the one door in the case that had ever been worth opening. He was sixty-something years old and had spent the length of a career learning which version you get by deciding too fast.

He drove back along the county line, the winter light going amber over hills he'd walked the length of twice in one lifetime, and wrote the evening's entry in the file — the first real entry since the reopened line, the one the box had been waiting for:

Confirmed: no county grading permit on the parcel's north section, 2008. Regrade unpermitted as well as undocumented. Foreman H. Colby: no county record after that season. Town account places his departure within days of the disappearance's peak week. Still living, retired, just past the county line the far side of the valley. Will go put the question to him before the festival; before the dirt on that parcel gets any deeper.

He read it back. It was the first time in eighteen years the file had moved with the momentum of a live case rather than the weight of a dead one, and he understood, sitting under the kitchen lamp with the dog asleep in its bed, that part of him had been waiting his whole retirement to watch that particular set of words sit down on a page.

He closed the file and put it away, and the ache in his knee told him rain was coming, the way it had been telling him for eighteen years, and this time he let it talk as long as it wanted.
