Chapter 6: What Emma Won't Say

She arrived an hour early, which nobody had asked of her and which was the only part of the morning she'd controlled. The site looked the way a groundbreaking looked in this part of the world: a shovel and a length of ribbon and a folding tent in case of weather, a caterer's van backing carefully across ground that didn't have a driveway yet, the county commissioner's sedan already squared away on the fresh gravel. A photographer from the Valley Weekly was walking the perimeter backward, trying to get the tent and the equipment into the same frame.

She got out of the car and looked at it the way she looked at everything, for what was out of place. The tree line came down off the ridge and took a full curve where the orchard used to be, and at the north edge of the cleared bowl the ground dropped away green and soft toward a pond she had never once walked to in the years she'd run the company. She knew the seam by heart anyway, from the maps in the same drawer as everything else: where the old bank had been graded down and the fill sown over with grass that had never quite taken root, the way disturbed ground never quite did. She stood looking at it a moment longer than she meant to, and told herself she was checking the sightlines for the tent, and then she walked down to meet Dennis, who was getting out of the sedan with his jacket on over his undershirt, the way he would commission a photograph to show because the morning was not his to be late to.

"Ready?" He put his hand out, and she shook it exactly once, firm, brief, a handshake's worth of handshake. She could have told him, from the grip alone, how many times he'd rehearsed standing at this ground. Eighteen years of it, by the feel. "Remarks at ten," she said.

"You wrote them," he said. "You just have to say them."

"Then it will be an easy morning."

Something flickered in his face — not doubt, exactly, closer to disappointment at being given no purchase — and he let it go, because at ten o'clock this was going to be his shovel in the ground, and he could afford to let her keep the secret of looking unhappy about it. "Your father's ground," he said. "He'd want to see it."

"He's the reason we're here," she said, and knew it was true in exactly the measure she'd arranged it to be true, and hated that the two of them could mean the same sentence so differently and she had to let both of them stand.

Priya came up behind them, not out of breath — Priya did not do out of breath — with the binder she'd spent a week building, the whole history of the parcel in plastic sleeves. "I found something stupid," she said, and held out a spread of clipped pages. "In the history packet. There's a foreman on the 2008 crew — Colby — and his records just stop. Mid-October. No exit form, no final timesheet, no reason. Like the payroll clerk dropped him between one page and the next."

She caught herself too fast, and knew it was too fast, because she had the name in her mouth almost before Priya got it out: Hank Colby, $7,426, October 6, booked to September. She had numbers before he finished the question, which was itself the tell, if Priya had known her well enough to read it. She made herself do the ordinary thing, which was look at the pages Priya was handing her as if she were seeing the name for the first time. "People didn't always clear out with paperwork in those years. Payroll was seasonal. My father ran a leaner office."

"He was your father, though." Priya took the pages back, squared them, and re-sleeved them, tidiness a kind of thinking. "I cleaned up files that old this week. This man was tidy. There's no other record in the whole parcel file that just stops. It's going to bother me."

The true sentence was standing behind her teeth, one word off, three words off, the sentence that would have explained the entire file in one breath, and she could feel herself not saying it the way you feel yourself holding a door against weather: He didn't leave, Priya. He was paid — The county commissioner called her name across the gravel, wanting her in the frame of the tent, and the photograph, and the morning, and the sentence stayed exactly where she had kept it for eighteen months, behind the one lock she trusted, and she turned toward the call with a smile she had trained for in a hundred property closings.

Faces arranged themselves around the shovel the way faces had clearly been arranged around committees, and the commissioner said the future of the county, and Dennis said the word vision twice, and then it was her turn, and she stepped up to the ribbon with the page of notes she had written twice and folded into three panels, and looked out at the faces and over their heads at the tree line, and began.

She had written: this ground has waited a long time to be built on. She had meant the honest thing by it, the only honest thing in the speech, the part about a town that had outlasted and the families that had kept it going. She got as far as "This ground has waited —" and the sentence came into her mouth wrong, all at once, carrying the weight of the map in her drawer and the seam in the grass and the check that had been moved to September so nobody would have to see it arrive, and she heard the pause go a beat too long, felt her own voice hold open a space it had not meant to hold open, and stood in it, looking at nothing, until her discipline came back and closed the door: "—has waited a long time for this day."

Nobody heard it. She folded the page and delivered the rest — the workforce, the county, the future on the ridge, all of it in her exact, pleasant voice — to a small round of applause she caught the shape of with her eyes closed, and thought, with the last of the space the pause had opened, I have never in my life been that close to a fact and pulled it back, except to you, Dad, and you're not here to leave it alone with me.

The shovel was ceremonial and the ground was soft and she put her hands on it because the board expected her hands on it, and the photographer aimed, and the commissioner said the future of the county a second time, and dirt — clean, dry, ordinary dirt — fell in a small and satisfactory arc, and everyone applauded, and it was done. She took one step back and watched her own hands go empty, a clean feeling with nothing in it, and thought, briefly, that the morning had gone exactly as scheduled, which someone would never know and everyone had just assumed.

She stayed after the cars left. It was the one thing she had not meant to do, and she caught herself doing it in the manner of a woman walking in her sleep — twenty minutes of the site, alone, after the caterer's van pulled out and the commissioner's sedan was just a dust on the county road. She timed it so nobody was in a frame of sight to see it. She walked north along the orchard edge, out from under the tent's shadow, until the ground began to drop and the seam came up under her feet and told her, in the way of a thing you have known for years from drawings, that she was standing where she had been standing in her own head since September of 2008.

This is the place. She could have the thought now, alone, since she had spent the entire morning refusing to have it. There was a moment — the ground under her soles gone soft the way backfilled ground goes soft, the grass on either side a different green, a fill line you could walk along like a scar — where the story she had never let herself finish was not a business risk but a fact, close enough to touch, held down only by the folds of the folder in a locked drawer a mile away. She stood in it that moment, feeling it fully, the way she had not allowed herself for eighteen months. And then, because she was her father's daughter and the skill hadn't stopped working because she'd seen through it, she reclassified the fact into a controllable category: a liability, defined, measured, priced, already moving through its budgeted timeline, exactly the kind of exposure a responsible operator carried and disclosed and managed. It was the most responsible position, protecting the most people, and she had taken it eighteen months ago and would take it tomorrow.

She turned around and walked back toward the car, and by the time she reached the gravel she had already done the arithmetic that said the folder could wait out the weekend, and the parcel could move, and nothing had happened today that she had been required to decide about — which she knew, standing on the newly turned ground, was not what had happened.

The drive back was the same road out of town that all the morning's cars had taken, the ridges going gray-green in the long autumn light, and she kept her hands at exact ten-and-two because it was the one thing about the drive she could do exactly. Her father's watch kept the time he'd set it to, the last morning he'd wound it, the same four o'clock, and the drawer would be there tonight, and the study, and the folder, and she had told herself the same thing at this same hour on four hundred nights and it had been true every time, right up until it stopped being true.

She didn't know, yet, which morning that was. It was not this one. It was, she told herself, going to be fine — the whole drive back into town in the comfortable arithmetic of daughter and employee and keeper of the name — and she believed most of it, the way she had believed it before, and the part she didn't believe she folded in with the rest of the unsaid things, into the same drawer, for the same reason, and drove the last two blocks with her hands at ten-and-two and nothing else moving.
