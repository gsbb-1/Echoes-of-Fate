# Chapter 12: Paths That Shouldn't Cross

She became aware, about halfway through October, that she'd started
looking for him — not looking in the way you look for someone you're
supposed to meet, but the smaller, worse habit of scanning a crowd for a
face you're not expecting and finding you're a little emptied when it
isn't there. It had been building for weeks without her permission, the
way everything lately had been building without her permission: the
tables at the café, the queue at the hardware, the bench outside Bennett's
where she sat to stretch her legs on market mornings. He was a stranger
who had once, twice, followed a painting to its source, and now he was
everywhere, which could mean only one of two things, and she was old
enough to know which one she wanted it to mean and young enough to let
herself believe it.

He was at the market again on Saturday. She saw him before she saw the
row of booths — same unhurried way of moving, same habit of looking at a
thing too long and calling it nothing, the way a man who'd spent his life
cataloguing exits would. He'd bought one of her small studies the week
before, the water one, the one she'd done at the gate, and it had made
her stomach drop when she recognized what she'd sold — she was not
supposed to be selling the place she couldn't stop painting, she was
supposed to be keeping it inside the walls where she could control the
light on it — and then he'd said, "This is the one I saw in the window,"
and she'd realized he'd been the one who kept coming back, and the inside
of her chest had done the thing the canvas did when she finally committed
the first stroke onto it, a kind of letting go she'd have had to name if
anyone had asked her, which nobody had.

"You again," she said, when he stopped at the booth's back edge. It came
out lighter than she'd meant it to, the way her voice came out light when
she was performing normalcy over something she didn't have words for.

"You again," he said, and she liked the flat of it, the way he said a
thing back to you exactly the way you'd handed it to him, no insulation.

She told him about the wall, because he'd asked about the piece, and
because telling him about the wall was a version of telling him she
might have something to show — the commission Owen had half-handedly
opened for her three weeks ago and that she'd been circling since. "He
wants something whole. Not the postcard stuff. Something I actually made."

"Do you know what it's going to be?"

This was the question, the one she was rehearsed against and had never
once — in almost a month of rehearsing — answered honestly. She had the
answer to it in three tries, the way she'd had the same answer for every
version of the question for three years, and the truth of it kept sloughing
off her like a color she'd undercoated and couldn't get to adhere. "I've
been going out past the lake," she heard herself say. "There's a place. I
keep coming back to it." She made it a small thing with her hands. "I
think it might be the place I've been painting. I mean — I think I finally
found where it is."

He had been looking at the table, at the stack of the same study in
smaller sizes, and he went still at the phrase *past the lake* in a way
she didn't catalog until later, when the pictures would have shown it to
her. "You found it," he said, carefully. "The place."

"I think so. It's an old orchard — or was. There's a gate and a county
road and the trees come down off the ridge and there's a long shine of
water at the far end. It matches the corner of everything. It always did.
I just never went to look at it before." She heard herself confessing to
a stranger in the middle of a market, which was not the day's plan, and
closed her mouth on the next sentence, which was *it feels like I've
stood there before* — too thin, always too thin.

"Maybe," he said, and something in his voice had gone exact, the way a
man's voice goes exact when he's testing the weight of a sentence he
hasn't decided to send, "you should be careful about that place. Some
ground costs more to walk than it looks like it does."

"I've been walking it for weeks," she said. "It's just ground. Probably."

He didn't argue, which she filed, because everything about him seemed to
come in the shape of things he hadn't said yet, and she went home with
the market takings lighter than the afternoon felt, the way the whole of
October had been going.

Her mother's voicemails had stacked up to eleven, which was a low number
for the season, and it was only that — the ordinary smallness of eleven —
that let her listen to one. The voice on it did what her mother's voice
always did, which was fill a room with the smooth machinery of the store:
your father's asking about the frost, the hardware order's come in, there
was a line at the register Tuesday like the whole town decided at once.
And then, in the same level, tidying register: "They're finally building
out the old orchard land, did you hear. About time it was worth
something, the way that ground was let to go. I always said —"

The sentence stopped there, the way a sentence in her mother's mouth
about that part of their history always stopped, mid-air, as if a door
had been closed on it from the other side. *The old orchard land.* Sarah
stood in her studio with the voicemail playing itself out into silence,
and the inside of her chest did a cold, quick thump, like the moment a
line you've been painting from memory finally finds the object it was
copying all along. The old orchard *was* the place. The gate, the
treeline, the shine of the lake — the parcel everyone in town referred to
as one fact — better than half of them had any cause to know it — was the
ground she'd been painting with her eyes closed. And underneath it, the
beater, the other pulse, went on: the cabin, the week, being carried,
raised voices somewhere she couldn't see and the water that was everywhere
in the memory and nowhere at all.

She stood there a long time with the phone hot in her hand, and then she
did what she always did with the stuff that lived in that particular
register of her body — she dropped it, the way you drop a hot plate
you know better than to hold, and she texted her mother something ordinary
back, *got the wall, going to hang the piece soon*, and put the phone
face-down, and went back to stretching the canvas she'd been avoiding all
week.

The gate didn't have a latch you could use from outside, which was the
point of a gate, and the NO TRESPASSING sign had gone silver the way
everything in this town went eventually, and Sarah
had learned to do her work in the margin of it — past the last fence
post, on the county road's own weedy shoulder, where the sightline opened
up over the track and the treeline and the long green bowl of the old
orchard all the way to the lake's far shine. She stood at the fence post
that Saturday afternoon shading her eyes against the low sun, canvases
in the truck, the gate-sketch from last week thumbed against her camera
roll, and the whole of it sat exactly the way it had sat every time she'd
come out here — real, and hers, and older than her, like meeting a
person you discover you've been quoting for years without ever having met.

She was setting up — easel, panel, the light already starting to go amber
off the ridge — when movement registered in the frame, and she looked up
and her heart did its emptied thing before her head caught up and she
recognized who was walking up the county road with his hands loose at his
sides, stopping at the gate like it was his to stop at.

"You found it too," she said, and it came out neither quite a question
nor a proper statement, and neither of them addressed the absurdity of
it, that the one place she'd come to work in peace was the one place the
stranger from the market also kept returning to, because the absurdity
was doing two things at once and she wanted to hold it that way.

"I come out here most days," he said. "I've been trying to find something.
I didn't know it was a something you could stand next to."

"I've been painting it for years without knowing it existed." She said it
like a confession, because it had started to feel like one. "I thought it
was nowhere. I told you it was nowhere. Turns out it's a real place, just
out past where everyone bothered to keep remembering it."

Something moved in his face, a failing she couldn't read in the amber
light — not the weight of a man being moved by art, though that's what
she'd have called it if she'd had to, something older and more careful
under it, the way a man looks at a door he recognizes from a story he
hasn't yet decided he's part of. "What is it about the place," he said,
and the question came out smaller than she thought he'd meant it to.
"Why this one."

She could have given him the practiced answer — the light, the negative
space, the way the treeline holds the ridge like a frame within a frame —
the professional's answer, which was true and which she'd rehearsed. What
came out instead was the other one, the one she'd never once said to
anyone: "I don't know. I've been painting it longer than I've been
painting anything. It feels like I've stood here before. Which is — I
was six. I have stood here before, probably. Doesn't make it a memory."

He was very still then, the way he'd been very still at the market, and
she watched him weigh a sentence he didn't send, and then he said, "I'm
Michael," in a voice that made it a real introduction rather than a
formality, as if he'd meant to say more and had said the small true
amount instead.

"I know." She hadn't realized she'd been keeping it — his name, that he'd
never once offered — but it surfaced then. "You never told me before."

"I'm telling you now." He looked past her at the treeline, at the long
shine of the water, and she watched him choose not to say the next thing
— twice. The first time he almost reached for it, and the second he
didn't. "I'd like to see the painting you're making of it, sometime."

"You're standing by it," she said, which was the direct thing and
terrified her, and he took it the way he took everything she handed him,
flat and without insulation.

"Then maybe I'll come back out tomorrow," he said, "and see it finished."

He left the way he'd come, along the county road, and she stood at the
fence post with her easel and her panel and the light going amber over the
place she'd finally found, and there it was, the thing she'd been
pretending for a month wasn't happening, made plain at last and made, by
her, on purpose: she'd stopped running into him and started standing in
front of him. The coincidence had resolved itself into choice on the hinge
of a stranger who kept ending up at the gate of a place she couldn't stop
painting, and she couldn't decide whether the strangeness of it was that
he'd been there all month or that she'd made a dogleg in her own life,
the way she'd dogleg a line on canvas when the straight one wasn't
honest, to keep ending up in front of him.

She worked until the light was gone and the study keyed itself together
almost without her — water first, the streak of the bank where the grass
had never taken, the pale long shine that was the lake and, glinting in
its length, something she'd been rendering for years without resolving it
into an object, a question she'd learned to paint around
rather than into. She set her brush down in the dusk, and for the first
time in three years she did not scrape it back to bare board.

She'd be back tomorrow. The man who kept finding the same gate she did
would walk up the county road and watch her finish it, and she couldn't
help the shape of it, walking home across the dark with the canvas
balanced against her hip like a passenger: that whatever she was making
for the café's back wall was already, whether she knew it or not, a
portrait of the two of them standing beside the ground they were both
keeping watch over — neither of them knowing the other's reason for being
there.
