# Chapter 17: What Sarah Finds Out

Owen aired the space the way he aired everything, by moving it around and
calling it a decision, and Sarah stood on a stool with the finished canvas
balanced against her hip and her knees unsteady in a way that had nothing
to do with the stool. The back wall of the café had been hers to claim for
six weeks, and now the whole town was going to walk past the finished
piece hung in the space she'd chosen so it would look inevitable.

"Up a touch on the left," Owen said.

"You've said that three times."

"Then it's true three times." He stepped back, head cocked, the way a man
cocks his head at something he wants to like. "It's the best thing that's
hung in here, Benny. I mean it."

He called her Benny when he was being sincere and Sarah when he was being
business, and she'd never once figured out which one she preferred, and
she tapped the nail a fourth time, for good measure, and climbed down and
stood beside him looking at it. Water first, the streak of bank where the
grass had never taken, the treeline coming down off the ridge, and the
pale long shine at the far end that she'd rendered for years without ever
resolving it into an object — she'd long since taught herself
to paint around it instead — and there it sat, hung, in Owen's café, for
anyone who cared to look.

She'd told him only that afternoon, at the gate, that she'd see it hung.
She hadn't told him the half of what she meant by it. And when the café
door opened on the sound of the four o'clock quiet no one walked into at
four, she knew without turning who it was, the way she'd known all month,
and Owen read the room the way Owen read money and drifted toward the
register, and Michael came and stood in front of the painting the way he'd
stood in front of the smaller one in her window a month ago, with his
hands at his sides and his whole attention on the ground in it.

He stood there long enough that the quiet built a wall around the two of
them, and then he said, level, the way he said things he'd decided were
facts, "It's done. It's really done."

"It's hung," she said, watching him instead of it. "That's different. One
of them's worse."

"Which one?"

"I'm pretending I don't know." She heard herself, the performed light, the
sound she made when something with weight was coming off the ground in
front of her, and for once she didn't like it on herself. She came around
the table and stood on his side of the painting, close enough that she
could see his face catch the light off the bank in it. "You've known this
place," she said. Not a question. "Before you knew me. You've known it the
whole time."

His stillness did the thing it did — the sentence he didn't send — and she
made herself be very quiet, which was the part of her that had learned to
let him take the time he took, and then he said, "I've been trying to find
it for eighteen years. I'd rebuilt it from a postcard and a description,
in my head, the way you build a place you've never seen. And then I
walked past your window, and there it was. Six square inches of it. The
treeline, the bank, the water sitting pale at the far end like it does. I
stopped in the middle of the street." He looked at her finally, and the
exactness had come out of his voice and left it plain. "You've been
painting where my brother disappeared. And I recognized it before I ever
knew your name."

She'd known, in the way you know a door is about to open from the other
side, and still the room did the thing rooms do when the floor moves — the
café, the wall, the whole of the months of her life going still at once.
She stood in front of the painting she'd made with her hands for
years, the ground under the treeline, the place she'd been standing at for
weeks, and let the sentence land and stay landed: the stranger who kept
coming back to her painting, who kept ending up at the gate, had been
looking at his dead brother's ground the whole time.

"You're the only person who's ever found it in the painting," she said,
and her voice came out steadier than she'd have bet on. "I thought it
was nowhere. I told you that. I told —" She stopped, and the fragment kept
going up in her like it always did, and for once, standing in front of the
thing she'd made of it, she said it out loud to another person, the whole
of it, in pieces: "I was six. My family had a cabin somewhere out past
the lake, and the week it happened — the week your brother — there was a
week where the adults were fighting about something I couldn't see, and
cold water, and being carried, and I've never once been able to say
whether any of it was real." She looked at the painting. "I've been
painting it longer than I've been painting anything. I thought that meant
it was nothing. It turns out it means the opposite of nothing."

Michael's face did the deep careful thing, the man reading a door he
recognized, and he said, "The cabin."

"We don't have a cabin. We've never had a cabin. That's the part I
couldn't ever make sit." She looked at him, and something in her chest
was turning over, the paint question finally keying to the thing it had
been painting toward all along. "My mother calls it the old orchard land.
The parcel out there. My mother — she talks about it like it was worth
nothing. She's always talked about it like it was worth nothing." She
picked up her coat. "I need to go ask her what that ground was to us."

"Sarah." He said her name the way he said most things, a fact he'd filed,
and when she looked at him he was still standing in front of the canvas
like a man guarding something. "Take your time with it."

"I've taken eighteen years of it," she said. "It's time I found out."

Bennett's Hardware had been closed for an hour. Sarah let herself in the
back the way she'd let herself in since she was tall enough to reach the
deadbolt, and her mother was in the back office doing the closed-up
arithmetic — the register tape, the order slips, the smooth machinery of
the store running so you couldn't hear anything else running under it.

"Mama."

"For a girl who's been gone all day, you found the place quick enough."
Diane Bennett didn't look up from the tape. "The piece get hung?"

"It's hung." Sarah stood in the office doorway and did not sit down,
which she knew her mother was reading as a signal, and didn't care.
"Mother. I need to ask you something, and I need you not to tidy it away
while you answer."

The register tape got a crease it didn't need. "That serious?"

"That serious." Sarah came in and sat on the edge of the desk, the way she
hadn't sat since she was a kid, small enough to matter. "The parcel out
past the lake. The old orchard land. The one they're building on. What was
that ground to our family?"

The crease in the tape got longer, and the smooth machinery of the room
went quiet a long moment, and Diane Bennett sat a long moment with her hands flat
on the desk and did not look up, and Sarah watched her mother do the thing
she'd done every time, for eighteen years — reach for the door to close on
it — and then, for the first time she could remember, not quite close it.

"That ground," her mother said slowly. "That ground was Hollis ground. My
daddy's ground." She said it like a word she was remembering from a
language nobody in the house spoke. "The orchard and the lake, and the
cabin up the track from it. Hollis ground, all of it, back to before
anybody in this town can remember anything. We had a cabin out there
still, when I was your age. I grew up in it half the summers."

Sarah's hands had gone still on her knees. "The cabin on the lake."

"You know about the cabin." It wasn't a question, and it was said with
the pause of a woman realizing a door she'd kept shut had a key out all
along.

"I don't know anything about it. That's the part." Sarah heard her own
voice, and it was steady, and it surprised her. "I've been painting that
ground for years, Mama. I can't stop painting it. And I never once knew
it was real, and I never once knew it was ours."

The room went still for a long moment, and then her mother did the
thing she had done perhaps three times in Sarah's girlhood, maybe twice:
she set the tape down, and she looked up. "It was a bad autumn," Diane
said. "The year you were six. Your daddy and I were just married, and the
store was a hope more than a trade, and Hollis ground — it was going. It
had been going a while, and it went all at once, and we sold it." The word
fell with a softness that was worse than if it had been hard. "We sold the
ground out from under our own family. The cabin, the orchard, the lake
frontage. All of it, to the Hale company, who needed it and had the money
to take it, the same week we were packing the last of the cabin out." She
made a small sound that might have been meant for a laugh. "I have never
once in eighteen years said that sentence out loud."

"The week," Sarah said, and her voice was barely anything. "The packing
up. The fighting. Being carried."

Her mother's face did something careful and old. "It was a hard week.
There was your Aunt Joan and your grandmother and the men from Hale's
crew coming and going with the paperwork, and the winter coming, and
nobody in that family saying goodbye to the ground because nobody could
say it. I carried you out of there myself, the last day." She looked down
at the tape, and back up. "You weren't supposed to remember any of it.
You were six. You weren't supposed to have any of it in you at all."

Sarah sat very still, and the fragment finally set the way sediment sets,
slowly, in the light of a whole different room: the cold water, the raised
voices she couldn't see, being carried down off the lake in her mother's
arms the week the family lost the ground — not a door shutting on a
violence, a door shutting on a leaving. She had been painting her family's
sold ground for as long as she had been painting anything, and she had
called it nowhere, and her mother had called it worthless, and neither of
them had ever once said the word that the other needed to hear.

"Hale Development," Sarah said. "That's who bought it. The same autumn."

"The same autumn, honey." Her mother said it like the autumn was two
words she was tired of. "Why you asking after it now? It's gone. It's
been gone. The whole town's been gone past it for eighteen years."

"Because the ground isn't the only thing that happened that autumn,"
Sarah said, and she picked up her coat, and she stood in the door of the
back office where she'd let herself for a lifetime, and she made herself
say the whole of what she now knew out loud, the way she'd made herself
stand in front of the finished thing and not run: "The man who went
missing out there. His brother is in town. And he's been looking at my
painting his whole life, and the same ground's been coming out of my
hands as long as I can hold a brush. And it was our ground, and we sold
it to the people at the center of it."
She looked at her mother across the small distance of the office. "I'm
going to tell him. He has to know what that ground was to us. To all of
us."

Her mother sat with her hands flat on the desk, and for a long moment
there was no machinery in the room at all, and then she said, quietly, the
way a woman says the thing she's held longest: "Then you tell him. And
you tell him from me — his brother never once set foot on ground that
somebody out of my own family didn't grow up walking on, before the
trouble came and took it." She looked at her daughter. "The ground is
gone, Sarah. But it was ours first, and that counts for something, if
there's any justice in the world."

Sarah walked home through the dark with the weight of it — the whole of
it, the cabin, the sale, Danny Voss's ground, the word *Hollis* sitting up
in her chest where she'd never once heard it before — and instead of
dropping it, the way she'd dropped every hot plate of her life, she
carried it. She was going to stand in front of the man who kept coming
back to her painting, and she was going to hand him the ground, the real
one, with her family's name on it, and see what he did with it. For
once, that was the whole of the plan, and it was enough.
