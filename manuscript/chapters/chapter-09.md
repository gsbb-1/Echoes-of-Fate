# Chapter 9: Two Jobs, One Boy

The diner shift ended at three and the offices started at seven, and
between them she had forty minutes she spent not standing still: the walk
home, the soup she'd left on low, the quick math of what Liam would eat
before the archive closed and what was left in the cupboard to make it.
The day ran on two tracks, the clock and the ledger, and somewhere
underneath both of them, all week, had been the shape of Jack Muldoon's
back in the corner booth with a man she didn't know.

She'd seen it happen. Three mornings ago, mid-shift, the old detective had
come in at his usual time and a stranger had come in behind him and sat
down off his elbow, and the two of them had moved to the back booth with
their heads down like men who didn't want the room to get a story going.
She'd found herself wiping the same stretch of counter three times over,
the way she'd found herself doing the same long stroke of the mop at the
offices on nights when her head wouldn't stay put. She hadn't looked at
them again and she hadn't allowed herself to wonder what a retired
detective and an out-of-towner had to say to each other in a booth, and
she'd gone back to the floor and the soup pot and the bus schedule, which
was how she handled all of it now, not looking, not wondering, moving
through the room the way she'd moved through it for eighteen years.

Not looking had been the whole system. It had kept her the job, kept her
out of his truck's headlights when he drove past the diner, kept her a
woman in a small town who worked hard and had a boy and no past worth a
question. It had worked. It was still working. It just cost more recently,
the way everything cost more recently, and she'd begun to notice the price
on the things she used to do for free.

The shift ended at three. Ruth took the counter over with a wave that
meant *go on* and she went, walking home along Main in the long autumn
light, and the bus that took her out of sight of the diner and the
detective and the booth was late by exactly her window, which was the way
the bus was and the way her luck ran, and she stood at the stop doing the
math on the week: what was in, what was out, what Friday needed, what
Liam needed, what she needed last and got last, the way she always did.
The account was what it was. It had never once in her life been what it
needed to be, and she had stopped keeping the difference in mind because
the difference was the shape living in the house with her.

At seven she clocked in at the Hale building with her kit, and the night
went the way the night went: the ground floor, the second, the corner
office that belonged to the woman who ran it all — Emma Hale, a name from
a kid who'd been a girl in her class, grown up into the building — the
desk she dusted around, the credenza she didn't open. She didn't open
anything. That was the rule she'd made at seventeen and never once
broken, in case some version of that night was sitting in a drawer
somewhere with her name printed on it; if you don't touch what's locked,
you never have to find out. The empty building hummed its empty-buildings
sounds — the fridge in the break room, the elevator's machinery yawn —
and she moved through it with the vacuum and the mop and the polish, the
whole of her attention on the work, which was the second best way she knew
to keep her head where her hands were.

The best way was Liam, and Liam was at the kitchen table when she got
home at nine-thirty, notebook out, phone in his hand, the light off in the
living room because the bulb was out and the two of them had gotten used
to walking around it. He didn't look up when she came in, which was
normal, and she let him have the full ten seconds before she said
anything, which was charity.

"Eat?"

"There's soup."

"The soup's been on since six." She hung her coat up, checked the pot —
low, fine, the way she'd left it — and set the table with two bowls
without being asked to set it with two, because that was the household
economy: she did it, and he didn't not eat.

He looked up then, and she saw he'd been waiting for her — the notebook
closed over something, his thumb holding his place — and he had the look
he got when he had a thing to show her that he'd decided was important,
and her whole body braced in the second before he spoke, the way it
braced at the sound of a car door out of place.

"So this is one of those town-history questions," he said. "For the
exhibit. We're digitizing the old papers, and there's this thing —"

It was going to be ordinary. It was going to be the festival, the
exhibit, the harvest fair, the town's goofy yellowed past, all of it
safe. She made herself let her shoulders down. "What thing."

"Missing person," Liam said. "This guy disappeared like forever ago.
There's this photo of the search — they looked
through the old orchard—" He was scrolling his phone, and she watched his
face the way she'd learned to watch his face, the held brightness of a
kid on the verge of being the one who found out something, and she knew
before the screen came up that it was going to be Daniel Voss, knew it the
way you know a date you've been dreading, because of course the archive,
because of course the one story in the whole town his hands would land on,
because the town was a drawer she'd never opened and her son was standing
in front of it with his fingers already under the latch.

"Here," he said, turning the phone to her.

The photo was gray and soft, the way old paper was soft. A long line
of volunteers walking shoulder to shoulder through the bare rows of an
orchard, heads down, spaced out across the frame like a fence being
dragged. The trees gone quiet. The low ground. The treeline at the top,
and behind the line a sliver of water catching the day.

She looked at it for maybe three seconds, with her hands flat on the
table, and did what she'd done for eighteen years: she absorbed it inward,
past her face, the way you take a hot plate from the counter — quick, so
it doesn't have time to show.

"Before my time," she said. "Terrible thing."

"You'd have been, like, my age. I figured you'd remember him. Everyone
old enough to remember him, the archive says—" He was watching her now,
studying her the way he studied her when he suspected she was doing an
adult thing at him, keeping something between them on purpose.

"I was a kid. Kids don't remember." She turned to the stove and stirred
the soup, which didn't need stirring, to give her hands something that
wasn't the table. "Anyway, it's a sad story. You don't have to make it
your story."

"Nobody said I was making it my story." His voice had gone flat the way
it went flat when he thought she was closing a door on him, which she was.
"It's just — it's interesting, is all. A guy whose whole town looked for
him. That's kind of something."

"It's sad," she repeated, to the soup pot, and heard how flat the two
words came out, and knew he'd heard it too, and knew he was sitting in
the light of it like a boy in the glare of a headlight deciding whether
to get out of the way.

He pushed his chair back. "Forget it. I've got stuff."

"Liam."

"I've got stuff, Ma. The soup's fine." The notebook went into his bag and
the bag went over his shoulder, and the last look he gave her was the
one he'd practiced on her all year, the one that said she was a door he
kept knocking on and getting no answer. It stung more than it used to,
because it was also true.

She let him go. She sat at the table in the dark living room with the
two bowls of soup going cold in front of her, and she didn't call after
him, didn't follow, didn't do the whole long practiced speech she'd built
in her head on a thousand nights about trust and safety and the difference
between them, because she'd never once in his life been able to finish
that speech without stopping at the one sentence she couldn't say.

She cleared the table. She washed the pot. She stood a long moment in the
doorway of her room with her hand on the light switch, and the bottom
drawer of her dresser was exactly where it always was, unopened, the way
it had been unopened for years, one object in it she hadn't once taken
out since she'd put it there, and she didn't open it, and she went to bed.

His room was on the other side of the wall, and after a while the
scratch and hum of his guitar started up — a tune he'd been working on,
the same four bars for a week now, trying to find the fifth one. She lay
in the dark with her son's five hundred yards of sound coming through the
plaster, and the fragment that had lived under everything since she was
seventeen came up the way it came up on bad nights, unbidden and
blameless: the dark of the treeline, the raised voices you couldn't
place, the splash and the silence after it, the truck lights swinging
down the track — and the running, the running, the years of running, and
the boy on the other side of the wall who didn't know he was finally
standing in the same orchard, looking down at the same ground, and did
not know what his mother was holding at her back to keep it from him.

The guitar stopped. The wall went quiet. She put her hand flat on the
plaster and said nothing, because saying it had never once, in eighteen
years, been a thing she'd been able to finish, and the boy on the other
side of the wall began, after a long moment, again.
