# Chapter 15: Closer

He came out the next day the way he'd said he would, walked the county
road from the direction of the motel and stopped at the fence post she'd
claimed, and stood there with his hands loose at his sides while she went
on with the canvas — which was, she realized, the most comfortable anyone
had ever been about it. The people who'd watched her paint before had
watched her. He watched the ground.

They did that for three days. She set up and he arrived, she made a
decision on a line and felt his attention make a smaller, exact version of
her thinking beside her, and twice he answered a question she hadn't
finished asking, which is how she knew he was holding the same picture she
was. On the second day he brought coffee and two cups and set one on the
fence post like an offering, and she took it. On the third day he told
her, out of the blue and aimed at the water, "You paint it like you're
remembering it, not inventing it."

"I told you. I've been painting it for years." She had the palette knife
going, working a clean line of bank where the grass had never taken.
"Longer than the place was real to me. There's not much to remember about
a thing you made up at six and never stopped trusting."

"Which is what I keep wondering," he said, "is whether you made it up at
six." He said it carefully, the way he'd said everything since the first
time she'd found him on the bench, testing the weight of a sentence before
he sent it, and she had the uncomfortable sense, as she had every time, of
being read rather than looked at. She wiped the knife and said nothing,
and he let it go, and the afternoon went back to the sound of wind in the
fence wire and the two of them keeping watch over the same ground — her
hand, and somewhere off his elbow the exact careful part of him that had
been watching the place, never quite her, since before they'd met.

---

She was the one who asked, finally, because it had been sitting up in her
chest for a month and the piece was almost done, and done things made her
brave. They were walking the weedy shoulder of the road back toward his
side of it, the light gone amber off the ridge, and she said, not looking
at him, "What are you even looking for? Out here. You said you've been
trying to find something. This is where you keep ending up."

He walked a few steps in the sound she'd made, then said, "My brother."
And then, because she'd finally asked, he gave her the whole of it, or
almost: "He was twenty-two. I was nineteen. He came up here for a summer
crew job, and he went missing in this town, and it was never closed. Not
for lack of trying — the county searched until they'd searched the place
out. They just never found him, or never found what happened."

She'd stopped walking without deciding to. "I'm sorry," she said, the way
you say it to a person opening a door you've known was there. "That's — I
didn't know. I mean, I knew the town — the case —"

"You've heard of it," he said. Not a question.

"Everybody here's heard of it." It came out gently, the way she'd have
said it to a stranger with a heavy story, except he wasn't a stranger
anymore and the weight of it was suddenly personal. "The historical
society's doing a whole exhibit on it for the festival. They've been at
the archive for months. The whole town's going to be reliving it whether
it wants to or not." She heard herself, a small-town leak, information
traded without thinking, and then she heard the rest of it catch in her
throat: the archive, the search coverage everyone had been hearing about
again for weeks. "Your brother," she said slowly. "He disappeared here."

"In this town," he agreed. He was looking out at the treeline, at the long
shine of the water at the far end of the bowl, and the exactness had come
into his voice, the one she'd learned to read as a door he was showing her
the edge of without opening. "I've been to the old detective. Jack
Muldoon. He kept the file. Nobody else in this town knows why I'm here,
and I'd like it to stay that way a little longer."

"Okay." It came out before she'd checked it for fear, and she watched him
hear the absence of it, and something in his shoulders settled — a man
who'd been braced, she understood late, for the moment he finally told
somebody and had it not cost him his plans.

"You're not going to ask me his name."

"I'm not going to push." She said it carefully, the whole of her attention
on the way the light was going across the bank. "But I'd find it out from
the town, if I asked around, and I don't want to hear it from the town."
She turned to him properly then, the measuring look, unhurried, the one
she'd given him on the bench outside the hardware a month ago. "So if it's
going to live between us, it should come from you."

He seemed to weigh that. Then he said, "Daniel. Danny, to us." A pause.
"Danny Voss."

The name did the thing names in this town did — stood up in her chest with
a shape she hadn't known she was carrying: the search, the exhibit, Jack
Muldoon's case, the old story everyone told in the plural, the way you tell
a thing you've stopped examining. She heard herself say, small and real,
"Voss." And then the connection landed, a stone dropped into the whole of
the last month: the stranger who'd kept coming back to the same six inches
of her painting, who kept ending up at the gate of the place she couldn't
stop painting, was the brother in the town's most guarded story. Beneath
it, quiet, the other pulse: the cabin, the water everywhere, being carried
— old, thin, unhandled, the register she never opened on purpose.

She looked at the water a long moment and almost said it — that this gate,
this bowl, this ground was where she'd been coming every day for a month,
that something in the picture wanted to slide home with his brother's name
on it — and then she dropped it, the way she dropped the other register,
the hot plate she knew better than to hold. She was very good at it. She'd
had a lifetime of practice. "Okay," she said again, and made it even.
"Danny Voss's brother, standing at my gate." A thin joke, her performed
light, and he took it the way he took everything, flat and without
insulation.

"It's why I come out here," he said. "I've been standing next to the only
piece of the ground he touched that's still walkable, waiting for it to
tell me something." He shook his head. "It's just ground. Probably." The
echo of her own words, handed back to her — *it's just ground, probably*,
from the market, a month of afternoons ago — and she felt her stomach drop
in a way that had nothing cold in it. "You're the only person here I've
said it to," he said. "The only person I want to say it to."

"Then you're going to owe me the whole of it, sometime." She said it
before she'd decided to, and it came out the way it sounded, like a door
closing on a hinge she'd chosen. "The whole of why, and who, and what you
think happened. When you can say it, I'm the one you say it to."

"Deal," he said, and the word came out like a man turning a key, and they
walked back to where the road divided, and at the gate he stopped and
said, "The painting's done, isn't it."

She looked back at the easel, the last of the light going amber across
the panel, and set the brush down on the rag with the small final sound of
it. "It's done," she said. "You noticed you were the audience."

"I noticed." He was looking at the finished thing, the water first, the
streak of bank where the grass had never taken, and something in his face
doing the deep careful thing it did over this ground, a man reading a door
he recognized from a story. "I'd want to see it hung, sometime. Where it
lands."

"The café wall," she said. "Before the festival." She said the word, and
felt the shape of it in her mouth — the festival, the exhibit, the town
gathering up its old ghost and putting it on public display — and came out
with, quieter, "Come see it there. That's where I'll be."

"I'll find you," he said, the way he said most things, like a fact he'd
already filed, and he left the way he always left, along the county road,
and she walked home in the dark with the finished canvas balanced against
her hip like a passenger, and all the way home the old thing kept turning
over inside her the way a paint question does when you've undercoated
your way into something real: she'd let the brother of the town's ghost
into the one place she kept her own ghosts, and instead of feeling wrong
the way every other person would have made it feel, it felt like the most
honest thing she'd done with her hands in years.

Her mother called twice that night. She let it ring out, and texted back
something ordinary — *the piece is done, I'm hanging it before the
festival* — and sat in the studio a long time looking at the finished
thing in the low light, the pale long shine at the far end that she'd
rendered for years without being able to resolve it into an object, and
she made herself a small, committed promise, the kind she usually
scattered as soon as it touched her hands: she was going to find out what
this place was, the whole of what it was, before she hung it. Starting
with her mother. She put the phone face-down and went to bed with the
promise still in her, which was new.

---

Michael walked back along the county road in the dark with his hands in
his jacket, and the last of the light went out behind the ridge, and he
let the thing he'd been holding all through the dusk finally have him:
he'd seen the finished painting whole, and it had been his brother's
ground. The treeline, the old bank, the north edge of the bowl where the
water lay flat and pale — he'd have known it anywhere. He'd spent
eighteen years learning the shape of it from a few lines written by a
woman who'd never been able to point to the place on a map, and Sarah
Bennett painted it the way a person paints a place they were born
remembering.

He hadn't told her. He'd stood in front of the canvas with his hands
going cold and had not told her, and then he'd made a deal with her he
intended to keep, and hadn't said the one sentence that would have
explained everything: *you've been painting where my brother disappeared,
and I recognized it before I ever knew your name*. It would have been
cruel, and it would also have been the whole reason, and he'd stood there
with the two of them on either side of it and chosen the smaller amount.

There was a glint in the bottom corner of the painting — a pale line where
the water met the bank, rendered like a reflection and refusing to
resolve into anything. He'd looked at it three times today. Each time
he'd nearly had it, a word in himself that almost surfaced, and each time
it had slid away from him, the way the town had slid away from him for a
month. He'd know later what it was. He knew, without being able to say
how, that he would stand in front of that canvas again, or one like it,
and the glint would come level with something in his head the way the
amber light had come level with the ground, and then he'd know the last
thing his brother had touched.

He turned off the county road toward the motel with the town settled dark
and orderly behind him, and the evening went on ahead of him, and he
carried her voice with him — *I'm the one you say it to* — and let
himself keep that much of the night unexamined.
