# Chapter 20: Liam's Reckoning

The first thing the remains did was make the old case real, and the second
thing it did was give Liam Marsh a reason to be late for the archive,
which was fine because the archive had stopped being a sentence from a
judge somewhere in the spring and had become the one place in town his
name was attached to something nobody was laughing at. He came in with his
phone out — the search photo, the bare rows, the line of men walking their
fence through the orchard — and the working folder on the workroom table
had grown a stack of the county's coverage since yesterday afternoon, the
exhibit folders opening like a person's whole life suddenly being talked
about out loud.

Mrs. Okafor was already at the accession desk, which meant she was
everywhere, the way she was when a folder mattered. She looked at the
phone in his hand like she'd known it would be there.

"Word's all over the county," he said. "People are saying it's him. The
missing guy."

"The state's said nothing yet," Mrs. Okafor said, which was the whole of
her way of agreeing. "The society's page is getting comments this morning.
Men who walked that ground are telling the story again. You're welcome to
keep at the digitizing — it's going to be a busy festival, and this
exhibit just became the most difficult thing in the building."

Difficult. That was her word for it, and he liked having a word. He sat
down at the scanner and picked up the rhythm, hum-click-clunk, and the
itch in his chest went double time, because the search photo was in his
pocket and the summer photo was on his phone and the notebook line was
in his head — *she's standing in both, still* — and the whole town was
suddenly standing in his mother's orchard.

He'd kept his mouth shut about it. That was the part that surprised him,
that a Marsh had held a discovery for all of October — him, who'd spent
his whole life at the front of every bad decision he'd ever made, holding
something back because a retired cop had looked at him and said *you keep
looking* and he'd taken it seriously, the way you take seriously the one
thing a person's ever handed you that was meant as a gift. He'd logged
the search photo, and kept his mother's photo in the box, and let the itch
sit. But the remains had changed the calculus of keeping anything, the
same way a fire changes the value of the things you were going to move
out anyway.

It wasn't a hard line to cross. That was the thing about a decision that
was a while coming: by the time he made it, it felt like remembering it.
The society's exhibit page had a login the digitizing volunteers shared —
dated photo accessions for the anniversary, Mrs. Okafor had said, nothing
personal, keep it clean — and Liam sat at the workroom computer with the
two photos pulled up side by side and typed the caption out slow, the way
you set a song's last chord:

*before the search: my mom, the orchard, 'the orchard, before' written on
the back. and the line that walked it that autumn. same place. two
seasons. she never mentioned it once.*

He looked at it. The comment section of his whole future self sat there
waiting for him to either hit post or close the tab, the way it had sat
waiting in October, and the same instinct rose up to close it — you don't
let the room get a story going before you understand the story — and this
time the itch lost its grip, because the room was getting a story whether
he posted or not, and he was tired of being the last person in the room
to have said anything.

He hit post.

He watched it collect — that was the thing, the small new thing he'd
lately started letting himself feel: it collected. A comment from someone
he didn't know, then a share, then the exhibit page's little counter
moving like a heartbeat. He'd been whoever-volunteers-were in this town
for a year, and here was his actual thing, his finding, his name on the
society's page, attached to the story the whole county was telling this
morning about the ground out at the orchard. He let himself have the
small heat of that for a moment, the way you let yourself have the first
thirty seconds of any good thing before you learn what it costs.

The account did the thing accounts do: nothing, for a minute, and then
the first comment in a voice he didn't know, *your mom?? the girl in the
second photo*, and then a share, and then a second comment from a name he
did recognize, an old man who'd been a fireman here, saying *never knew
the Voss boy had a girl in town* — and Liam's hand went still on the
mouse, because that was the first time anyone had said the name into his
actual thing.

The third comment came from a woman whose profile picture was a dog, and
it said, *wait. the fella at the edge of the first photo. that's him. the
one they're looking for at the pond.*

Liam sat very still in the workroom with the hum of the scanner going and
his own comment section suddenly a room he was standing in alone, and he
opened the exhibit folder and pulled the October coverage — the picture
the county papers had run, the young man's face, the jaw, the way the hair
lay against the forehead — and he held it up beside his phone, his
mother's summer field at the edge of frame, the young man in the work
shirt half cut off by the corner, the way some people were always mostly
out of whatever picture they were in, and the two faces sat level with
each other, and the click went through him like a chord that finally
resolved:

the man at the edge of his mother's kept photograph was the missing one.

His mother had been standing three yards from him, in the orchard, in a
kept photo, with a felt-tipped note on the back that read *the orchard,
before* — and she had never once, in his whole life, mentioned a man like
that, or an orchard, or a summer, or anything that had a chance of
explaining a girl in a t-shirt standing at the edge of a field where the
town's most famous search line had walked.

He looked at the screen, and the comments were multiplying, a third a
fourth a fifth, and Mrs. Okafor was going to see it, and the whole town
was going to see it, the way they'd seen everything since the remains —
and somewhere upstairs in his chest the feeling that had been the itch for
months turned over and showed him the shape it had been hiding the whole
time: *she knew.* His mother had known the man they were finding at the
pond. She'd stood beside him and smiled a smile she almost never wore in
real time and she had never said one word to her own son about any of it,
and he had just handed that secret to the town on the society's account,
the same week the ground gave the man back, the same way he'd handed over
everything this year — loud, and proud, and too fast to take back.

He sat with the click still going through him, and for the first time in
his life he looked at his mother the way you look at a person you've
discovered was a person before you: the two-job back, the diner days and
the office nights, the bus, the shoe-box past with the felt-tip notes,
the way she'd gone flat when the old missing-man story came near her at
the counter — *this town's lost a lot of people, kid* — smooth as a
register, with her hands on something else. He'd never once wondered
what was under the smoothness. He'd figured out, at seventeen, that his
mother had a past he wasn't part of, and filed it the way you file a
parent's childhood, somewhere you don't visit. He was visiting it now,
in the workroom, on the society's page, with the comments stacking up
under the photo of her smile.

She was going to see it. Not today, maybe not tomorrow — his mother
didn't spend her coppers on the society's page — but it would reach her
the way everything in this town reached her, through the register,
through Ruth's voice, through a customer who remembered a face. And she'd
find out, from the town, that her son had laid her old picture down next
to the only man she'd never once mentioned, and she'd have to decide, in
public, what to do with the state of him having done it.

He reached for the keyboard to take it down, and his hand stopped, because
his actual name was already on it, and Mrs. Okafor's account was already
sharing it, and the old fireman had already said *never knew the Voss boy
had a girl in town* into a page that had, by a quick count he didn't want
to finish, been looked at eight hundred times since the remains. Taking it
down was just making a story look like a secret, which was the one thing
a story liked more than being told.

He sat in the workroom a long time with the scanner humming and the
comment section growing, and the itch he'd carried for months had gone
quiet in his chest, replaced by the particular silence of doors closing on
frames he'd walked through without looking. He'd wanted, his whole life,
for one real thing he found to be taken seriously — wanted it badly
enough to have burned down small pieces of himself on it all year — and
here it was, serious as a county seal, serious as the remains in the
bank: his finding, out of a box in his mother's closet, had more weight
on it this afternoon than anything he'd ever made. He should have known,
the way his luck ran, that the weight would be his own family's. The old
man's voice went through him, the blessing he'd stood under in this same
room — *you keep looking* — and he understood, sitting there, that Jack
Muldoon had meant it for a different kind of boy, one who could look and
put it back down.

The post was out. It was living in a public place now, and his mother was at the diner halfway
across town with her back to the room, wiping the same stretch of
counter, having no idea that her son had just set her picture down in the
middle of the town's headline next to the only man she'd never once
mentioned.

He put his phone face-down on the workroom table, the way you set down a
thing you've just learned you can't put back, and the scanner went
hum-click-clunk, and somewhere out past the lake, in a sealed county room,
a man's name was waiting on dental records, and every strand of the
whole town's oldest story was suddenly, finally, on the move — and the
boy who'd started it from a box in his mother's closet sat alone in the
archive, trying to find the fifth chord, because he was holding the
answer to a song he hadn't known he was writing.
