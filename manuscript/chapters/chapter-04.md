# Chapter 4: A Name Across the Room

His savings had stretched into a third week, longer than he'd planned,
and the motel did what motels did: nothing, neatly, twice a day. The
coffee in the room was a reheated shadow of coffee. The showerhead needed
a washer. The window looked out on the parking lot, the highway, and the
low green flank of the valley beyond, and he'd watched all three from the
same chair, the map unfolded across the bed, the postcard propped against
the lamp — a red circle thumbnailed onto it by his mother eighteen years
ago, a place neither of them had ever actually been.

He kept the room because keeping it forced him to do what he'd come here
to do. For eighteen years the rule had been: no town gets more than a
season's worth of Michael Voss. He'd kept it the way a man keeps a promise
he made to himself in a parking lot at nineteen — by never staying
anywhere long enough to be asked a second question. Willow Creek had
already asked several. He could feel the town the way you feel weather
coming, a place full of people who'd start to remember him on purpose if
he stayed.

He'd stayed anyway. That was the part he kept circling, carefully, without
touching.

He hadn't planned on asking anyone. Asking was how a stranger stopped
being background noise and started being a story people told each other
at the diner counter — *there's a guy been asking about the artist, you
believe that* — and Michael had spent eighteen years arranging his life
so that no town ever got enough of him to tell a good story about. But
he'd been back to the café window twice more since the first morning, and
both times the painting had said the same thing to him it said the first
time, which was nothing he could translate into a next move on his own.

So he asked. Quietly, at the counter, to the kid working the register,
nodding at the propped canvas in passing like it was an afterthought.
"Who does the paintings in the window?"

"Sarah. She's in a lot, actually — you just missed her, like, ten
minutes ago." The kid didn't look up from the register. "She does the
market on Saturdays too, if you want to actually buy something."

Michael thanked him and didn't leave. "Does she work out of town?" He
meant where the paintings came from — there was a chance, if she painted
from a reference photo, that the image was of somewhere else entirely,
some real water with a real name he could stand at and file next to
nobody.

"Nope. She's always here." The kid finally looked up, then, at Michael's
jacket, his hands on the counter, the two ways out of the room, and
whatever he saw there pulled the sentence up short. "She's got a show
coming on the café wall," he said, into the lull, like offering the
town's weather as a peace treaty. "You could come to that. Whole wall's
gonna be hers."

Which was more than Michael had asked for and less than he needed, and he
took it anyway, the way you take a thing you'll sort later: Sarah, the
market, a show, a whole wall — none of it matching a woman who painted a
place that didn't exist. He was still holding it when he stepped out onto
the sidewalk and saw her, two blocks down, on a bench outside the hardware
store.

He didn't want to buy anything. He wanted to know why a stranger's
painting had six square inches in it that matched, almost exactly, an
image he'd been building for eighteen years out of a postcard and a red
circle drawn by a woman who'd never once been able to point to the place
on a map.

She was sitting close
enough to the window display that he understood, a beat late, that she
must work there too, or her family did — Bennett's, the sign said, in
paint gone soft at the edges the way everything in this town seemed to
round off eventually. She had a sketchbook open and wasn't
drawing in it. She looked up when his shadow crossed the page, and he
watched her place him — not as a stranger, exactly, but as *the* stranger,
the one from across the street, the recognition arriving on her face in
about the time it took her to decide whether to be alarmed by it.

"You're the one who was staring at my painting," she said. Not a
question. "Twice, that I saw."

"I liked it," Michael said, which was true and also not remotely the
reason, and he heard how thin it sounded even as he said it.

"People who like a painting usually look at the whole painting." She
closed the sketchbook, unhurried, a small deliberate motion that told him
more than her voice did — she wasn't scared of him, she was deciding
something about him, weighing it the way you'd weigh a stranger who'd
followed you two blocks, which, he had to admit, standing there, was
roughly what he'd done. "You kept looking at the same corner. Both times."

"The water," he said, before he'd decided to say it.

Something in her face changed — not fear, closer to what his own face
probably did when someone asked him a question he'd rehearsed not
answering. "It's not water. It's negative space. It's a technical
choice."

"Okay."

"You don't believe me."

"I didn't say that."

"You didn't have to." She looked at him properly then, for the first
time, the way he imagined she looked at things she was about to paint —
cataloguing, unhurried, faintly clinical, and he had the strange
sensation of being read rather than looked at. "Are you buying something,
or is this a different kind of conversation?"

He should have said *buying*. It was the easy door, the one that let both
of them go back to their afternoons with nothing owed. "I'm looking for a
place," he said instead. "Near the water. I don't have a name for it. I
was hoping the painting did."

"It's not a real place."

"You sound very sure for someone who called it a technical choice thirty
seconds ago."

That landed somewhere; he watched it land, the small flinch she covered
by opening the sketchbook again, not to draw, just to have somewhere to
put her hands. "It's not anywhere," she said, quieter. "I've been painting
it for years. It doesn't mean anything."

"Things that don't mean anything don't usually make people flinch when a
stranger asks about them."

"You're not exactly forthcoming yourself." She said it fast, a
redirect, and it worked, in the sense that it was true — he had told her
nothing, not his name, not why a corner of paint on a tourist commission
had stopped him on a public street twice in one week. He weighed handing
her a piece of it, a small one, the kind of true thing that cost him
almost nothing, and found he wanted to more than the situation strictly
called for.

"My brother went missing near water," he said. "A long time ago. I've
never seen the place. I've been trying to find it since I got here."

She went very still. It wasn't the reaction he'd braced for — not
alarm, not the polite retreat of someone realizing they'd been talking to
a stranger with a heavy story. It was closer to recognition, the specific
stillness of someone hearing a name for a shape they'd been carrying
without one. "I'm sorry," she said, and meant it, and didn't ask the
obvious next question, which he noticed, and filed.

Her not asking was worse than being asked. Eighteen years had stocked him
with prepared sentences, the correct temperature of *my brother, missing,
we were close*, and most people reached for one — they wanted the
news-story version with the edges tucked in. She hadn't. She'd gone still
long enough to hold what he'd said, the way you hold a plate with an
uneven surface, set it down, gone on talking to him like he was a person,
which was its own kind of alarm. He'd caught himself wanting to tell her
the rest — the postcard, the map, the red circle, the years — and that
want was at least as large as anything he'd come here to get, and he
filed that too.

"You don't have to be sorry. I just thought — " He didn't finish it.
There wasn't a version of the sentence that didn't sound like an
accusation aimed at a woman who painted the same six inches of nowhere
without knowing why.

"I should go," Sarah said, standing, sketchbook against her chest like a
shield she hadn't meant to need. "I have a piece to finish."

"The show," he said, and watched that land too — she hadn't told him
about the show, he'd overheard it from the kid at the register along with
her name, and she clocked exactly what that meant about how much
information he collected without asking directly for it.

"You really don't do the easy version of a conversation, do you," she
said, not quite a smile, not quite not one.

"No," he said. "I don't."

She left without giving him her number, or asking for his, and he stood
outside Bennett's Hardware longer than he needed to, watching the door
she'd gone through, filing that too — the flinch, the *I'm sorry*, the
question she hadn't asked — into the same place he kept everything he
hadn't found a use for yet.

He took the long way back, down the side streets where nobody was, because
he wanted to be the only one who saw his hands come out of his pockets
empty and go back in. The postcard had made the walk from the motel room
into his jacket without his deciding on it, and at the corner where the
side street met Main he stopped under the bank awning — the same reach of
shade he'd been using all week, for reasons he was getting less interested
in explaining to himself — and took the postcard out and looked at the red
circle with the light going sideways across it.

There was a version of this where he put it back and kept walking. On the
highway by dark. Willow Creek, like the towns before it, holding the faint
impression of a man who'd stood under its awnings and left nothing in
writing. He'd done that leave a dozen times, and the leaving got easier
each time, which he'd always told himself was progress.

He did not put the postcard back.

He had the feeling, walking down Main Street with the postcard folded
square in his pocket, that he'd just done the thing he came to this town
specifically not to do, which was become someone that a person here would
remember.

He found, turning it over, that he didn't mind as much as he should have.
