# Chapter 1: Six Lives, One Town

The light came in low and gold through the east window, the way it did
maybe a dozen mornings a year, and Sarah had learned to drop everything
else when it showed up. She had twenty minutes of it, if she was lucky.
Fifteen, more likely. She mixed a warm gray fast, wet into wet, laid it
along the roofline of the piece she was supposed to have finished
yesterday — a commission, three cafés on Main Street rendered in the kind
of loose, sunstruck style that sold well to people who wanted Willow Creek
to look the way they remembered it rather than the way it was.

It was going well. It had been going well for an hour, which was usually
the point where she stopped trusting it.

She noticed the water before she noticed she'd painted it. Bottom left
corner, where the third café's awning should have been throwing shadow
across the sidewalk, there was instead a paler passage, cooler than
everything around it, with a horizontal band cutting through the gray that
didn't belong to any building on Main Street. She set the brush down. She
picked it back up. She stood there with it not touching the canvas for
long enough that the wet edge of the gray started to skin over, which
meant she'd have to fight it now instead of blending it.

She didn't remember deciding to paint that. She never did.

Her phone lit up on the drafting table, face-down, buzzing once and then
again, insistent in the way only one contact in her phone was insistent.
She didn't turn it over. She knew the shape of the voicemail she'd get if
she let it go to voicemail — three days from now there'd be another one
under it, and another, a soft stack of *just checking in* and *your father
was asking* that she'd let build until the number in the corner of her
screen looked like an accusation. She picked up the brush instead, scraped
the pale water-shape back to bare canvas with the flat edge, and started
the awning over, her jaw tight, the good light already starting to change
color as the sun cleared the ridge.

Owen wanted the piece by two. She had, if she didn't stop again, maybe
enough time.

She almost did stop again. Twice more before ten, her eye kept sliding
back to the corner she'd painted over, the way a tongue kept finding a
sore tooth, checking that the bare canvas was still bare, that nothing had
come back while she wasn't looking. It hadn't. It was just gray board and
a base coat now, ordinary as anything. She made herself work the far side
of the canvas instead, the window of the bookshop, the particular green
she liked for old awning canvas gone soft with weather, and by the time
her phone buzzed again — Owen this time, not her mother, *on my way, tell
me it's dry* — she had almost stopped thinking about the water at all.

Almost. She wiped her hands on the rag tucked into her waistband and
looked, one more time, at the spot low in the corner where nothing was.
There was a version of this where she told someone about it — that for
three years now, whenever her hand got ahead of her head, it painted the
same six or seven inches of some place she'd never been, dark water and a
paler line breaking the surface of it, over and over, in the margins of
things that weren't supposed to have room for it. She didn't have anyone
to tell. She wasn't sure, if she tried, it would come out as anything
other than the kind of thing people used to explain why an artist needed
a break from painting rather than more of it.

She heard Owen's van pull up outside, and put the rag down, and went to
let him in.

Jack had a system for the box, and the system was: he wasn't going to open
it today.

He made coffee. He fed the dog, who was fourteen and mostly slept, and
took his own cup out to the porch to drink while the dog didn't bother
following him. Tuesdays he walked into town instead of driving, forty
minutes round trip that his physical therapist had approved of and his
knee tolerated if he stopped once on the way back. He liked the diner for
the walk as much as the coffee. He liked, if he was honest with himself,
having somewhere to be that wasn't the house.

Ruth had his order going before he sat down. Two stools over, Pete
Alcala and one of the younger guys from the county roads crew were
talking with their heads close together in the specific way men talked
when they had a piece of news and were enjoying being the ones who had it
first.

"—finally moving on it," Pete was saying. "Crew's out there this morning,
staking it."

"The lake parcel?" Ruth said, not really asking, sliding a coffee down the
counter without looking.

"Eighteen years they've sat on that ground," Pete said. "My cousin did
the original survey work back when Hale bought it. Now all the sudden."

Jack's hand found the handle of his own cup before his mind had finished
catching up to what his ears had already understood. He didn't say
anything. He'd learned a long time ago, in this town, in this job, that
the fastest way to end a piece of information was to ask a direct question
about it — people closed up the second they clocked you as wanting to know
something rather than already knowing it, out of habit, the way water
finds the low ground. He drank his coffee. He let Pete keep talking, half
of it, into the roads-crew guy, who didn't much care and said so, and by
the time Jack put money on the counter he'd gotten a corroborating hour —
this morning, crews already staking the ground — without having asked a
single thing.

He walked home slower than he'd walked in. Stopped once, per doctor's
orders, though the ache in his knee wasn't really why.

The box was where it always was, in the hall closet behind the winter
coats, an ordinary banker's box gone soft at the corners from eighteen
years of being taken down and put back. He told himself, taking it down,
that he was only going to check a date. He was going to look at one page
and put it back and that would be that.

He was still standing there with the lid off when the dog finally got up
to come see what he was doing.

The file was thinner than people probably imagined, when they imagined it
at all. Eighteen years of a cold case looked, on paper, like less than a
week of an active one — a search log, three witness statements he could
still recite from memory, a handful of photographs going soft at the
corners the way old photographs did no matter how carefully you kept
them. He didn't take the statements out. He knew what was in them. He
found instead the single sheet he'd actually come down here for, the
plat map somebody at the county had given him back when this was still an
active file and he still had the standing to ask for things, the old
parcel lines drawn in a surveyor's careful hand over the orchard and the
lake and the low ground between them.

He set his coffee cup down on the closet shelf, out of the way, and spread
the map flat against his knee, and looked at it the way he'd looked at it
probably four hundred times before, checking nothing in particular, just
looking, the way another man might have gone back to church.

Whoever was out there staking ground this morning was standing on paper
Jack had memorized before some of them were born. He didn't know yet
whether that meant anything. It rarely had, the other times. But it was
Tuesday, and he had nowhere else he needed to be, and the box was already
open.

Olivia had the soup going and the bus schedule running in the back of her
head at the same time, which was how most of her mornings worked — two
tracks, the thing in front of her and the thing she couldn't put down.
Eleven minutes until Liam needed to be out the door. Six hours until her
shift at the diner ended and the four at the offices began. She did the
math on what was in the account and what was due out of it by Friday
without meaning to, the way some people hummed.

Her phone buzzed against the counter. Liam.

*running behind. staying after at the archive thing anyway. don't wait up*

She read it twice, which was one more time than it needed. It was nothing.
He'd signed up for the community-service hours at the historical society
weeks ago — some box of old newspapers nobody had gotten around to
scanning — and staying late to actually finish something was, if anything,
a good sign, the kind of thing she should have been glad about instead of
turning over twice like a stone with something underneath it she couldn't
name. She didn't have the fifteen seconds it would take to figure out why
it snagged. She had eleven minutes and a pot that needed stirring and a
son who, lately, told her less than he used to and expected her not to
notice.

*ok. eat something,* she typed back, and put the phone face-down, the way
she put most things face-down that she didn't have room for yet.

She was halfway through explaining to no one in particular why the bus was
always exactly four minutes later than the app said it would be when she
heard it: a low mechanical groan from somewhere out past the edge of town,
faint enough that she almost mistook it for a truck out on the highway.
It went on a beat too long to be a truck. Then it stopped, and started
again, steadier this time, the particular grinding hum of something big
being woken up after a long time asleep.

She didn't know what it was. She didn't have time to wonder. She turned
the burner down under the soup, checked the clock, and went to find her
shoes.

The diner was already half full when she clocked in, Ruth working the
counter alone and grateful to see her, the morning crowd loud in the
particular way it got loud on a Tuesday when half the men in it had
somewhere to be by seven and were running late getting there. She tied her
apron on the move, took table six's order without writing it down,
refilled Pete Alcala's coffee without being asked and caught the tail end
of whatever he was telling the roads-crew kid — something about the lake
parcel, Hale finally moving dirt after all these years — and let it pass
through her the way most morning talk did, in one ear, gone before she
reached the next table.

It was only later, restocking creamers, that she noticed her hands had
gone still over the little tub of them, and that she couldn't have said
for how long.

She shook it off. There wasn't room in a Tuesday for whatever that was.
She finished the creamers, checked the clock — four hours to the end of
this shift, ten to the end of the next one — and went back out onto the
floor, because table six was waving her down and the coffee wasn't going
to pour itself.
