# Chapter 7: Cold Case

Jack had the diner down to a catalog, the way he'd carried a beat for
thirty-one years and the way he still carried his own mornings: coffee
truck before seven, Pete Alcala in his stool by eight-fifteen on a good
day, Ruth behind the counter with the coffee poured before he reached the
register. In a town this size the door itself was a tool — the regulars'
door, the day-tripper door — and you could read the morning from which one
opened, and the stranger came through the regulars' door and read wrong
immediately: no jacket, no greeting for Ruth, no check of the menu board,
and, after a pause that was supposed to look casual, one stool down from
Jack, leaving the seat between them empty, which was a buffer and a
tell in the same gesture.

A man who walked into a diner he'd never been in and sat without ordering
was either lost or there to talk to somebody already in the room. Jack let
the coffee get one more sip ahead of him and waited.

"You're Jack Muldoon."

"Depends who's asking."

"Michael Voss." The man said it like he was filing a complaint. "Danny's
brother. You worked his case."

Jack hadn't heard the name on the air in years, not the way this man meant
it — the family name, the one the town said softly and then moved past.
"You worked a lot of cases" was what he'd meant to say, the standard
retirement-floor deflection, the one that had walked him out of a hundred
conversations at the hardware store. What came out instead was: "Eighteen
years."

"I know how long it's been. I'm not here to hear you tell me."

"Then what are you here for?"

Michael Voss had the particular stillness of a man who'd rehearsed a
conversation more than he'd had it. He was younger than the case by a
long way, mid-thirties, and he watched Jack the way Jack had watched
people for a living — for tells. "People in this town remember you," he
said. "I asked around. They told me you kept the file. That you never
really let it go."

"They tell everybody that." It was true, and it was also an exit, and
Michael Voss didn't take the exit.

"They told me you take it down," he said. "They told me you still update
it. Eighteen years."

Jack set the coffee down. It was a Tuesday, and his knee was already
serving notice of weather to come, and he was sixty-something years old
and had been carefully, deliberately not thinking about the box for the
length of a movie since the Tuesday last week. "Why don't you tell me
what you actually came to say," he said. "I'm not the town's tour."

Michael Voss looked at him a moment. Then he did the thing Jack couldn't
have predicted and would later realize was the only thing that could have
worked: he took a cardboard sleeve out of his jacket and set it on the
counter between them, flat, like a man putting down a hand he meant the
table to see.

"Sixty-two cents," he said. "My mother's kept it in her kitchen drawer
for eighteen years. It's the only piece of him that's had a paper trail
this whole time, and your department never saw it."

Jack turned the sleeve over without touching the card. Plastic was always
a step too far in the chain — kept it cold, kept it your own. He could see
the picture on the front: the lake under the ridge, a shoreline dotted
with cabins, the photograph gone soft at the edges the way every piece of
this town went soft eventually. He didn't open it. "You understand I can't
just — "

"Read it," Michael Voss said, and there was something in the word that
didn't match the filed-complaint delivery — the same note you'd find in a
man asking a favor he was afraid of. "Read it and tell me if I'm wasting
your time and mine. That's all I'm asking."

Jack looked at Ruth across the counter. Ruth was pretending not to watch
them, the way she'd pretended for thirty years. He looked at the man in
the aisle seat waiting for him to be reasonable, and then he took the
card out of the sleeve and opened it, and he read it, and the world did
that thing it only does when a door you've spent years learning to stop
standing in front of opens on its own.

The front of the card was the lake. The back was a postmark his eyes
found first — the week before the date he knew better than his own
birthday, the week before the search lines went out — and then a few lines
in a steady, unselfconscious hand:

*Working the old orchard ground north of the lake. Found something out
here Hale's not going to like. Tell you when I'm back. — D*

He read it twice. The first time, the way you read an old case — for
sequence, for place, for the soft outline of someone's last week. The
second time he read just the one line, the one with a name in it, and he
heard his own voice go flat and careful, which was what his voice did
when his hands had gone cold and he needed the rest of him to keep the
light in the room exactly level.

"Your mother told us he wrote home," Jack said. "She never brought us the
card."

"She couldn't. It was his handwriting. She said if she gave it up it
would be like agreeing he was gone." Michael Voss was watching him now —
reading the reading — and something had shifted in his face. Less
complaint. More like a man holding his breath. "We're not wasting your
time?"

"Not yet." Jack slid the card back into the sleeve the way you'd put a
photograph back in a frame — careful, one piece — and set his hands flat
on the counter on either side of it. "Not by a long way."

The counter was a stage. He'd known that during the election years, during
every investigation, during the town's two generations of looking at him
sideways over pie: you wanted a private conversation, you took it to a
booth, and you took it before the regulars finished making a story out of
the two of you. Jack stood and led Michael Voss to the corner booth by the
back window, the one with its back to the room, and Ruth advanced with
the coffee pot like she'd been given a signal.

Behind them, from the corner of his eye, he caught the shape of Olivia
Marsh at the end of the counter, wiping the same stretch of counter three
times while her attention sat somewhere across the room. He didn't follow
the line of her attention. He'd followed the line of Olivia Marsh's
attention at eighteen, in an office with a tape recorder and three separate
witness-statement forms, and he had spent eighteen years learning not to
reach for that particular thread again, because it had never once done
anything but slip, and it had cost him whatever the department had left to
lend him at the end.

In the booth, Michael Voss put his elbows on the table and told him things
Jack hadn't had in the file. That Danny had taken the crew job that season
because it was the first time their family could swing the ride out to the
lake. That he'd been talking, in the letters home, about the land like it
owned him, "old orchard ground, told me it was going to be something." That
the family had a story, which Michael had spent years not trusting, about
their grandfather's land and a boundary dispute that had never been put to
bed, and Danny had gone to Willow Creek with his head full of it and had
started asking questions out there, and nobody in the family could have
said who he'd asked. Jack listened the way he'd always listened — for what
was being careful, and what was being careful in the other direction — and
filed the grandfather-story next to the postcard, marked loosely, to be
looked at with clean hands later.

"I went out to the place when I got here," Michael said. "There's a gate
and a no-trespassing sign old as the hills, and crews started up out there
last week like they own the calendar. They're not going to let a stranger
walk in and read his brother's ground."

"You're going to need to ask people questions eventually," Jack said.
"That's how it works. You'd be amazed how much of a cold case is just
who's left to ask."

"Then you're going to ask them."

"I'm going to read my notes with the one piece of paper I didn't have.
That's as much of a promise as I make."

It hung there a moment. Then Michael Voss nodded once, the way a man
nods to close a deal he isn't sure of, and stood, and Jack watched him
pick up the fall of his jacket and noticed, because he noticed what was
out of place the way other men noticed faces, that the watch on the man's
wrist was stopped — cracked face, hand frozen at 4:47 — and that the man
wore it anyway, like a man might wear a dead man's coat because the
pockets were already his.

Michael Voss had reached the door before he turned. "She gave me the card
to bring with me," he said. "I'm at the motel off the county road. If you
find something in the box, you know where I am."

"I know how to find you," Jack said. "This town doesn't lose people."

He watched Michael Voss leave through the regulars' door, and Ruth came
and collected the coffee cups without asking, the way she'd learned to
collect cups at the tables of men who were about to go do something she'd
be hearing about later.

"Trouble?" she said.

"No trouble," Jack said. "Just old."

He paid and walked home slower than the doctor liked and faster than he
should have, one stop on the way, the knee talking to him the whole way
about weather that was still a day out. The house was the same as he'd
left it — dog asleep on the porch, lights off, the long evening stretching
out with nothing in it but the memory of how to be alone. He did not go
to the hall closet right away. That would have been going to the hall
closet. Instead he made himself pour a glass of water and stand at the
kitchen window with it, looking at the ridge going gray against the
coming rain.

None of which made the closet any less where he ended up, on a Tuesday,
in a house where the only sound was the refrigerator settling.

He took the box down with the postcard still in its sleeve still in his
hand, and he put the sleeve on the lid of the box before he opened it,
and he stood there with his hand on the cardboard and did not tell
himself anything at all, because the last two weeks he'd told himself he
was only going to check a date, and this time that was a lie he wasn't
going to have the nerve for.

The lid came off in his hands as if it had been waiting for him to
remember the way.
