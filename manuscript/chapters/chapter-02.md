# Chapter 2: What the Morning Hides

Michael had been in town four days and still hadn't found the place. He
had a photograph on his phone, a scan of a scan, an old aerial shot with a
red circle drawn on it in a hand that wasn't his — his mother's, from
years back, a circle she'd drawn around a stretch of shoreline based on
nothing more than a line in a postcard and never been able to get closer
to than that. He had directions from a gas station clerk that had gotten
him as far as a county road with no sign on it, and a motel room with a
window unit that cycled on and off all night, and a postcard in a plastic
sleeve in his jacket pocket that he touched, without meaning to, maybe
twenty times a day, the way another man might touch a phone in his
pocket to make sure it was still there.

*Found something out here Hale's not going to like. Tell you when I'm
back. — D*

He knew the postcard by memory. He carried it anyway.

Main Street ran two blocks and then thinned out into the kind of
in-between road that could turn into anything — houses, then a gas
station, then nothing — and he walked it slow, the way he walked any new
town, cataloguing exits without deciding to: the alley between the
hardware store and the bank, the bus stop with actual posted times, which
door of the diner people were using and which one they weren't. A truck
went by loaded with equipment he didn't recognize, moving with purpose
toward wherever the road thinned out to. He filed that too, without
knowing yet that it mattered.

He stopped because of the window, not because of anything else. A café,
half a block down, glass painted gold by the morning sun the way every
window on the street was painted gold at this hour, and in it, propped on
a little wire easel among the pastry case and the chalkboard menu, a
painting. Three storefronts rendered loose and sunlit, competent,
pleasant, the kind of thing a tourist would buy without thinking twice.
He wouldn't have looked twice either, except for the corner of it — low
left, a passage of cooler gray that didn't match anything else in the
piece, water where there shouldn't have been water, a pale horizontal line
breaking the surface of it.

He stood there long enough that a woman coming out with a coffee gave him
the look people gave a man staring too hard at nothing. He kept walking.
He didn't have a name for why his chest had gone tight looking at six
square inches of somebody else's painting. He filed that too, the way he
filed everything, and told himself he'd think about it later, which was
what he told himself about most things.

He asked at the gas station again, a different clerk this time, younger,
who at least knew what he meant when Michael said *the old orchard land,
out past the lake* instead of trying to describe the red circle on his
phone. The kid gave him a road name and a rough mileage and then, unasked,
a piece of information Michael hadn't known to want: "That's the Hale
parcel. They're breaking ground out there — like, this week, I think. My
uncle's on the crew." Michael said thanks and didn't ask anything else,
because asking a second question was how a stranger stopped being someone
passing through and started being someone people talked about, and he
wasn't ready yet to be talked about.

He drove out along the road the kid had named, got as far as a chained
gate and a NO TRESPASSING sign gone silver with age, and stopped there,
engine idling, looking down a dirt track he couldn't see the end of. Past
the treeline he could hear something — an engine, heavier than his own,
starting and stopping in the particular rhythm of machinery being tested
before a real day's work. He sat with his hands on the wheel and didn't
get out. Not yet. He'd come this far without a plan past *find the place*,
and the place, it turned out, had a gate and a schedule and men on a crew
who weren't going to let a stranger in just because his brother's postcard
said something ugly about the man who owned it.

He turned the car around. There'd be another way in. There was always
another way in, if you were patient, and if there was one thing eighteen
years had taught him, it was that.

The call was already running four minutes over when Emma said the number
out loud for the second time, which was usually the tell that a call
wasn't going anywhere new.

"Q3 carrying costs on the lakeside parcel are eleven percent of
projected," she said, "against an eighteen percent contingency we built in
specifically for a delay like this one. We are not behind the model. We
are inside the model." She had the figure before Dennis from the board
finished asking for it, which was itself a kind of answer, if he'd known
her well enough to hear it that way. He didn't. Nobody on the call did.
That was, mostly, the point.

"Eighteen years is a long time to be inside a model, Emma."

"Eighteen years is how long it took to clear title cleanly on three
separate easements your father's board wouldn't have touched," she said,
which was true, and which was also not the reason, and she said it in the
same even register she'd have used for either one, so that the difference
between them didn't show.

She watched, through the glass wall of her office, the floor outside go
about its morning — Priya on the phone with a contractor, the new
assistant refilling the coffee station, the ordinary machinery of a
company that had outlived the man who built it functioning, as it always
did, without needing her permission. The equipment was out there this
morning. She knew because she'd approved the permit six weeks ago,
because she'd been the one holding the line on the timeline as long as
there'd been a line to hold, and because today, for reasons she hadn't let
herself finish the sentence on, the line had run out.

"We break ground Thursday," Dennis said. "The board would like to see you
out there for it."

"I'll be there," Emma said, and her hand went, without her telling it to,
to the watch on her left wrist — her father's, the leather band replaced
twice since he'd worn it, the face still keeping the time he'd set it to
the last morning he'd wound it. She'd meant to stop wearing it a dozen
times. Taking it off had always felt like a decision, and she'd never
quite been ready to be the one who made it.

She ended the call and sat for a moment with her hand still on the watch,
looking at the bottom drawer of the credenza across the room, the one
with the lock her father had never explained and she'd never asked about
until it was too late to ask him. She didn't open it. She had a Thursday
to get ready for.

Priya knocked once and put her head in — a name to confirm for the
groundbreaking press list, a caterer to approve, the small logistics of
turning a permit into a photograph of a shovel in the ground — and Emma
answered each question crisply and completely and watched her own hands
do it, steady, competent, exactly the hands a company this size needed at
the top of it. It was, she thought, not for the first time, a genuinely
useful skill, being able to run the machinery of herself from some
distance away while the rest of her sat somewhere else entirely, waiting
to be let back in.

"You good?" Priya asked, on her way out, the kind of question people asked
without expecting more than a yes.

"I'm good," Emma said, and believed roughly half of it, and turned back to
her screen before the other half could find a place to land.

The archive room at the historical society smelled like a library that
had given up trying to smell like anything else, and Liam had exactly
twenty-one boxes left of the sixty-two his court date had turned into, and a
system going: pull the folder, flatten the pages, run each one through
the scanner, log it, next folder. It wasn't terrible. Nobody talked to
him for two hours at a stretch, which suited him fine, and the scanner had
a rhythm to it, a hum and a flash and a soft mechanical clunk, that he'd
started hearing as a beat without meaning to, counting folders the way
he'd count bars.

Box forty-one was mostly obituaries, dull as anything, until it wasn't.

*LOCAL MAN STILL MISSING, SEARCH ENTERS THIRD WEEK*, the headline said,
above a photograph that had gone the particular brown-gray of newsprint
left in a box for two decades — a line of volunteers walking shoulder to
shoulder through the orchard, spaced out across the frame like a drawn-out
chord. He wasn't reading the article. He didn't especially care about a
missing guy from before he was born. What snagged him was the photograph
itself, the composition of it, something about the shape the search line
made cutting across the rows of trees that he couldn't have explained and
didn't try to — it just sat wrong in a way that felt like it wanted
looking at again, later, when he had more than a scanner's worth of
attention to give it.

He didn't scan it. He set it, instead, on top of the finished stack
instead of the flattened one, which wasn't the system, which he'd have to
explain if anyone checked, and pulled the next folder.

Mrs. Okafor, who ran the archive and had a way of appearing in a doorway
without having audibly walked there, clocked the stack out of the corner
of her eye on her way past with a box of her own. "Find something good?"

"Just some old missing-person thing," Liam said. "Guy never turned up,
looks like."

"Voss." She said it without having to think about it, the way people who'd
lived here forever said certain names — not sadly, exactly, just worn
smooth from use, like a step everybody knew to avoid the creak on. "Before
your time. Before mine, almost." She didn't stop walking. "Log it if
you're going to move it. I don't care what order, I care where it ends
up."

"Yeah. Sorry." He didn't log it. He put his phone against the corner of
the photograph instead, quick, badly framed, and told himself it was for
later, for no reason he could have named if she'd asked, and went back to
folder forty-two, the scanner's hum picking back up under his hands like
he'd never stopped.

By the time he noticed the sound outside — a low engine groan, then
quiet, then a steadier grinding hum that didn't sound like anything usual
for a Tuesday — he'd already half forgotten the headline, his mind back on
the scanner's rhythm, four folders left before he could tell his
supervisor he was done for the day. He didn't look up to place the sound.
He had somewhere to be by four, and a stack that wasn't going to log
itself.
